When I was looking for a painting to share with someone to do an art writing exchange – where we each write about a painting we choose for each other – there was one painting that stood out to me that I wanted to analyse: Picasso’s [The Poet (Le Poète)](https://www.guggenheim.org/artwork/3429). The painting, in Cubist style, was cryptic at first but, after reading the description, studying the painting, and talking through the work with a friend (thanks [Naty](https://burgeonlab.com/)!), I became ever more curious and wanted to write my thoughts.

The title stood out to me: The Poet. When I look at the poem with this title in mind, I see the complexities and potential depths of poetry: the difficulties of trying to find a word that precisely encapsulates an experience or a moment. I see this in all the straight, dark lines that appear throughout the painting, and in the cacophony of disparate shapes that come together to create a bigger picture in which, every time I look, a new detail comes to my attention. The lack of colour makes me think that the poet is having a difficult time: alone with their thoughts, the colour is fading from them. The poet appears to be looking down, too: perhaps an allusion to introspective thought.

A curved line surrounding an oval in the centre of the top quarter of the painting marks the beginning of the face, which I first noticed after reading and re-reading the [Guggenheim’s description of the painting](https://www.guggenheim.org/artwork/3429). The description goes on to note how, if you follow the features of the face, “a complete image can be reconstituted by the inference of chin, pipe, neck, attenuated torso, elbows, and chair arms.” I am not sure if I have been able to create a complete image of the poet in my mind, but, with study, I noticed the two arms on the side, of the painting – straight arms. I can see what might be an elbow bending in the bottom left of the painting. It is as if the poet’s hands come together in front of them, as if clasped. But I can’t see the other hand yet.

This painting leaves me with a lot of questions. What is the significance of the gap in the middle where there are no shapes? Is the shape in the top left corner a window? The top half of the painting appears lighter than the bottom half. Is there a light shining onto the poet? A light of inspiration? But then why do they have their back turned to the light? Why is the painting rendered in so few colours? A poet is capable of representing many colours. This may hint at the kind of poetry this particular figure writes; perhaps more solemn poetry.

“The Poet” is a painting of contrasts: of light and dark, of lines and curves, of abstraction and, upon further study, revelation of form and structure. This balance between abstraction and revelation makes me think of poetry itself: sometimes, it takes a few readings to understand a poem. So too has it taken me quite a bit of time to begin to understand this piece, to unpack the layers, to notice details.

I am fascinated by how the title has influenced how much I receive this piece. I would not have seen the figure in the painting as a poet without the title being “The Poet”, but now I have that information much of what I see and understand is based on the figure being a poet.

I really love this painting.
