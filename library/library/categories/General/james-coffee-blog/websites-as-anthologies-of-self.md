At this month’s [Homebrew Website Club Writing Edition](https://events.indieweb.org/2026/07/homebrew-website-club-writing-edition-m2NVbVuF8wqT) (notes to come; will be shared likely on July 12th), the topic of audience came up. Who is the audience for our website? Do we have multiple audiences? Do we think of audience at all?

In amongst this discussion, I started to define a distinction between the author themselves being an “audience” and the audiences of others (a website can have many audiences that may be discrete or overlapping). This is an alternate framing of the idea of writing for one’s self, but I think goes further in that it implies some degree of follow-through after a work.

As the author of this website, I use my site in a different way, I suspect, from readers. I use my site for creative reference. I use my site to remember if I have covered a topic before. I link to posts that I want to build on as I develop an argument in future posts. And, over time, my website becomes something of an anthology of self.

My website documents the seasons of my life: [the fact that I think of Winter in summer](https://jamesg.blog/2026/07/11/winter), [my affinity for whimsy](https://jamesg.blog/2026/05/31/milk-chews). It documents the moments when, in hindsight, something changed, like when I wrote “[Little moments of joy](https://jamesg.blog/2023/02/16/moments-of-joy)” in 2023, in which I developed a [practice of noting moments that bring me joy I am still using today](https://jamesg.blog/2026/07/11/joys).

Every post here is atomic in the sense that it captures a moment in time, while also continuing a narrative I have been writing for years. A single post on a given day may not add to the previous one, but it adds to the more global picture of my self and who I am as a writer. I am starting to appreciate the temporal properties of blogging more – in particular, that posts have dates. The dates add significant context to a piece. They give order to my anthology.

When I write multiple posts in a day, I am now relatively sensitive about publishing them in the order in which they were written. This is because I find that posts sometimes follow on from one another, even in subtle ways. Preserving the order in which each post was written when it is published means that readers can see the story as I experienced and wrote it.

Over the last few months I have wondered about doing longer pieces of writing – on the order of several thousands of words. I think setting this as a goal would let me access certain affordances in the writing process: I would be forced to go deeper on topics. With that said, it doesn’t feel like the right time. The framing of my website as an anthology of self, however, creates the impression that every piece adds to a bigger picture. Maybe I’m not writing a novel all in one, but instead writing it over years. I am writing as I live.

On the topic of chronology, during the meetup I shared that I have previously written blog posts when a specific flower outside blossomed. When I want to know if the plant has bloomed early or late or on time, I have been able to look back to my archives for my notes. [Mike](https://m9z.org/) pointed out that by virtue of writing about Nature, my blog represents some of the patterns in Nature. I wonder if this is true for others as it relates to their areas of interest, too – do you see patterns in the kinds of things you write about? How has the story of your website changed over the years – how has it changed with you?
