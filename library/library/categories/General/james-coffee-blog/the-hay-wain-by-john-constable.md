*V.H. and I are doing a painting exchange challenge where we each choose a painting for the other person to write about. We gave each other three options. From the options given to me, I chose The Hay Wain by John Constable.* [*V.H. wrote about Corot’s “Entrance to the Wood”*](https://vhbelvadi.com/entrance-to-the-wood)*.*

Writing about a painting that was voted as one of the “[greatest paintings in Britain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_Painting_in_Britain_Vote)” feels rather daunting, but that feeling melts away when I look to [John Constable’s *The Hay Wain*](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/the-hay-wain) and let myself spend a moment in the Suffolk countryside.

When I first saw this painting, I didn’t know what the “hay wain” referred to. Reading the painting description on the National Gallery website, I now know this refers to the cart that is in the centre of the image, which would go to pick up hay from the field in the background. And yet I found the painting just as enchanting when I first saw it as I do now. The scene is so peaceful. We don’t need to know all the details to see the beauty in something (although more details do help illuminate different pieces of a work).

Whenever I look at this painting, I feel calm. I smile. I enter into a pastoral scene where I can imagine the slowly trundling wain (cart) moving through the water, making my favourite sound – running water – as it goes to collect and deposit hay. The colours are relaxing: the realising greens and the blues of the sky and the water, the light grey of the cart, the whites of the cloud.

On the left side of the painting, there is a cottage; in the middle, there is the river Stour, on the right, there is a wide field from which hay was being farmed. The cottage looks like it would be a lovely place to live. The river looks like an ideal place to look out to when you need a quiet moment. The painting feels relatively still, even though there is work actively going on to gather hay; a snapshot of a moment in time.

Nature takes up most of the painting: tall trees over the left side of the river, the fields, the expansive sky, the trees in the background. With that said, on close study, figures start to appear. There are the two people operating the wain, as well as a man in the right foreground who could be a fisherman, all the people working the field in the background, and a woman who is reaching into the river on the left side next to the cottage. There are also animals: the horses pulling the cart, the dog in the foreground, the ducks on the left side next to the empty boat that floats in the water near the fisherman.

I am struck by how flat the countryside is on the right side of the painting. Here in Scotland, I am almost always surrounded by hills, and so every time I see a flat plain of land in the countryside my eyes open. There is flat land here too, but I am so used to hills.

I can see why *The Hay Wain* was voted one of the best paintings in Britain in the aforelinked 2005 BBC Radio 5 contest. The scene is calming. The artwork reminds me of the British countryside. As I write, I have an image of *The Hay Wain* up on screen next to the window and there feels to be some degree of continuity – not exact continuity, rather the kind you feel when the essence of something has been captured so well that you can see the connection between what you know and what has been painted.

Before I wrote this post, I read some of the background material from the National Gallery on the painting. Three pieces of information. First, the name “The Hay Wain” is derived from a nickname given to the piece by Archdeacon Fisher in letters to Constable. I have always wanted to learn more about how paintings get their names, so this piece of information was especially interesting to me. Second, *The Hay Wain* was painted in Constable’s London studio, with reference to prior sketches he had made of the scene. It is amazing how a work that so beautifully captures. Finally, in discussing reactions to the painting, the National Gallery noted the significance of looking to Nature for inspiration (references removed, see “[Discussion](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/catalogues/egerton-2000/the-hay-wain)” section on the painting “From the Catalogue” description for the full text):

> According to Delacroix, Géricault returned to France ‘quite stunned’ by Constable’s picture, while the romantic writer Charles Nodier rhapsodised over it in his Promenade de Dieppe aux Montagnes d’Ecosse, published within a few months of the exhibition: singling out Constable’s picture, he suggested that French artists should similarly look to nature instead of solely to the ‘voyage de Rome’ for inspiration.

While I think inspiration can be found anywhere and there is merit in travelling to see new places for inspiration, I do also see there being so many stories to tell from Nature; I have never tired of looking at paintings from and of Nature. Constable did a terrific job at capturing a moment by the river Stour in Suffolk, a slice of Nature and humanity and industry all in one place. I’m still smiling at this painting. I can see why the work is so well loved.
