*This is my second music exchange with V.H. (*[*see our first exchange*](https://jamesg.blog/2026/08/28/lost-in-yesterday-satisfied-mind)*) in which we each shared songs with each other from which we would pick one to write about. I chose “Bridge Over the Stars” by Keiko Matsui from the options V.H. sent me, and V.H. chose “iPod Touch” by Ninajirachi from the options I sent him.* [*You can also read this page on V.H.’s website.*](https://vhbelvadi.com/track-exchange-02)

Bridge Over the Stars by Keiko Matsui – James
----------

Keiko Matsui's Bridge Over the Stars, opening with a somewhat ominous note from an instrument I cannot identify, develops a drum beat for ten seconds before the piano comes in. When I hear the start of the melody, I want to rush to my piano to try and learn it; the notes might be too advanced for me to play because I don’t usually play jazz, but the melody has a resonance that leaves me curious.

Twenty seconds in, I feel the music more, swaying with the melody that will repeat several times throughout the song and the consistent beat from drums in the background. The song, which I heard on an album called “Smooth Jazz #1’s”, certainly lives up to the descriptor “smooth jazz.”

Around 0:40, the music takes on a melancholy mood for a moment longer, before the melody comes back in again. I continue to sway with the music. At 1:10, a short sequence of high notes capture my attention, before the melody repeats, as soothing as the sound of a voice I know well speaking softly.

At 1:31, high notes play again. It marks a change in the song. The song develops. Now, I hear chords more distinctly being played, chords that fit in well with the initial melody. Then, at 1:48, there is a new, short, repetitive pattern that takes over for ten seconds. Following this, the background takes centre stage for a few moments, with the occasional piano note whose presence grows as time passes – a moment of relative quiet in the middle of the song. By 2:22, the piano is resuming its more prominent role. I want to close my eyes and appreciate all the notes.

At 2:45, more high notes play, this time as part of the melody rather than feeling as if they are a discrete part of the song as was the case earlier. Then, at 3:06 the pianist cascades up the from low notes to high notes. This was unexpected, but delightful. At 3:35, I stop and feel as if I hear something familiar. I check back and hear a similar pattern to the one played at 1:47.

Four minutes in, the music starts to fade out.

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

I don’t often listen to jazz but, when I do, I love songs that have a few words to which I can sing along, like [I’m Old Fashioned](https://jamesg.blog/2026/08/17/im-old-fashioned) which I listened to as part of a music exchange with Ruben. Bridge Over the Stars, however, has no words: sounds and melodies drive the song. With that said, from the opening melody, I felt as if the song had a certain “groove” that had me in its grasp. I love this song.

I am curious about the contrast between the ominous sounds at the beginning and the piano playing. The combination works, but it is a little unexpected. This has me thinking about the title of the song: Bridge Across the Stars. What does it mean for there to be a bridge across the stars? Is the artist traversing long distances? Does the beginning of the song represent the clouds through which one would go before reaching space and seeing the stars? The song sometimes feels melancholy. Does this refer to the loneliness of the distance one would travel if one were crossing over stars? Do the high notes represent the stars themselves? When the piano briefly takes on a less prominent role at \~2:00, ceding to the backing instrument, does this represent the person crossing the bridge taking a moment to reflect reflecting?

The title has me thinking about how much context a title adds to a painting or a written work: a title can say something that informs how someone sees the artwork. My musical analysis skills are not developed enough to know the extent to which a title can tell a story. Perhaps all of my questions above are redundant, and me impressing a fictional world I am envisioning from the song. Nevertheless, the song does leave me fascinated, and reflecting more on what titles mean to me in my own creative pursuits.

I do wonder: why did the artist choose the title “Bridge Over the Stars”? Whatever the reason, this song was a joy to listen to. Thank you V.H. for offering this as one of the options for me to study for our music exchange.

iPod Touch by Ninajirachi – V.H.
----------

This collaboration has made me realise a couple of things: one is that there are some genres I have steered clear of in my music library, modern EDM being one of them; <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-1"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#1">1</a></sup> the other is that I judge music by its covers, specifically tracks by titles. A cursory look back at my recent additions to my library tells me that the only time I have added oddly titled tracks to my collection is when I heard the song accidentally. Now add a second reason to that: when I had to listen to it as part of a track exchange.

I am pretty sure that if I had stumbled upon a track named after a modern-day gadget performed by an artist named after a Pokémon I would have moved on. In my defence, there are more songs today than I can possibly discover and I need some method of culling them initially. With ‘iPod Touch’ by Ninajirachi – whose real name is Nina Wilson – I would have missed something quite interesting. Of course, Nina is one of a new generation of artists who grew up in the 2010s, by which point having laptops was perfectly normal, even expected, and who therefore naturally came to view it as a tool suited to every step of music production. She calls her record ‘computer music’ which I really like. It makes making music feel more democratic than it actually is, not to mention accurately describes what ‘iPod Touch’ itself happens to be.

‘iPod Touch’ is a wonderful combination of trance-like upbeat synths with lyrics that speak, in warped vocals, of a time for which a millennial like me holds no nostalgia. But you do not have to go so far into the song to be enraptured by it: for me it was the first two lines that sold the entire track—

> *I’ve got a song that nobody knows  
> I put it on when nobody’s home*

This is a brief moment of universality in a track that wears its decidedly 2010s theme on its sleeve. I have no idea if this was intentional but starting boldly with an idea that everyone who enjoys listening to music can connect with was a clever choice. Now of course we do not believe any song is a *real* secret but we all have those tracks that are *ours* and that we keep to ourselves as though nobody would ever find out about them if we never revealed them.

It is such moments that keep you listening closely to the track and before long your mind has filtered out the 2010s you do not connect to and made you appreciate parts of the song you *do* recognise. To say that there is something for everybody might make this track sound like a try-hard amalgamation of glimpses from the past. It is much more than that. It has a distinct direction, a fine-tuned intentionality, to it. Still, the universality is what really elevates it for me: random songs with boosted bass (this was a very common 2010s trope but you might say Nina’s references are different from my interpretation <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-2"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#2">2</a></sup>), stashing your portable music player under your pillow, downloading FL Studio or other such mixers, or listening to songs with your headphones on the bus back home.

But no matter: talking about lyrics in an EDM track, some might argue, points to misplaced priorities. The real question is whether the track makes you want to dance. I think the answer is an emphatic *yes*. There is plenty of reverb, a continuous stream of unmissable – almost frantic – beats that accompany the entire song, and with it a sizzling stream of echoes. There is nothing in the music that really stands out, and it ends almost as quickly as it begins, all of which is by design. There is similarly a lack of vocal intonation into which Nina leans, further pushing the song into computerised territory, making it reminiscent of an overclocked CPU. These unconventional choices make the song unapologetically itself while simultaneously keeping it in line with lots of early 2000s electronic pop.

‘iPod Touch’ makes its unusual-sounding vocals central to the track in much the same way – but technically differently to – fellow Australian musician Tones and I on tracks like the famous ‘Dance Monkey’. While the latter split the crowd, I see ‘iPod Touch’ doing nothing of the sort. It is a comparatively more conventional track and is all the better for it. There is a certain sense of properness in singing an ode to computers and music using EDM and doing so through a track so clearly shaped by a computer. It is computers all the way down. Until we reach a time when these devices are so old that we can serenade them with a string quartet without it feeling odd, Ninajirachi’s hooky ‘iPod Touch’ will probably remain the quintessential embodiment of 2010s techno-nostalgia.
