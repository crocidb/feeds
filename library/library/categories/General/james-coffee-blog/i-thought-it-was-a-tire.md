I’m participating in [Blaugust](https://nerdgirlthoughts.game.blog/2026/07/15/blaugust-2026-is-coming/) this year. Like most things, on a whim.

Nerd Girl, who’s coordinating this year’s edition, compiled [community-builder prompts](https://nerdgirlthoughts.game.blog/2026/07/23/blaugust-2026-calendar-weekly-prompts-group-projects/). I’m always for those, because where would we be without community?

One challenge: write a guest post for someone’s site, or have someone write one for yours.

I thought: why not both?

If you’re wondering why the writing style here sounds different, that’s why! This is [James’ website](https://jamesg.blog/). I’m is Zachary Kai, and mine is [zacharykai.net](https://jamesg.blog/zacharykai.net). We’re exchanging posts.

I asked James if he’d write about how he sees art, because [he’s studying art history](https://jamesg.blog/2025/11/12/studying-art-history), and the more I hear about art history from other people, the better.

He asked me to write about nature, fitting, since that’s what I enjoy most about what he writes.

(I dictated this, so it’s rambly. At least I can clean it up in post, as a film person might say.)

Two Kinds Of Country
----------

I’ve had many experiences with nature. Haven’t we all? That’s the way we live.

When in Australia, I don’t live on the coast. I live in a semi-arid (dry) region, in a rural part of [Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_\(state\)), the state at the bottom of the main island.

In the pandemic’s later years, I lived in the coastal [Northern Rivers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Rivers) region of New South Wales.

I’d highly recommend visiting. The folks are lovely, the place is stunning, and it was an enormous privilege to be living there. It was so quiet.

I saw things I’d never seen before in my lifetime, and perhaps never will again.

A Detour About Masks
----------

I’m grateful the worst of it is over. It was terrible for everyone involved.

Sometimes I look at the measures we took then and question myself. Why am I not extending people the same courtesies now? Why does it take a crisis for us to do that?

I’m not saying I’m any better. On plane flights, when I actually remember to wear one, I’m one of three people in a mask. I think: even if nobody else on this plane is immunocompromised, if you have a cold, isn’t it a kind thing to wear a mask? So the mother going home to her kids and straight back to work after a business trip doesn’t have to spend a week in bed?

I don’t know.

It was only a few years ago, which feels incredible, and shows you habituation’s power.

I hesitate to say nature *taught me a lesson*, because that framing is awful. The pandemic was a tragedy, and turning it into a ‘growth’ opportunity (*look what I learned!*) isn’t what I mean at all.

What I mean is it reminded me things’ll often work out if we don’t take such a grip onto them.

Let me explain, because I know that sounds ridiculous.

The Turtle In The Rock Pool
----------

That coastline had several beaches side by side, each with a ecosystem. One was known for a specific plant life, the next for something else. It was fascinating how crossing one bluff, or one series of rocks, gave you a different expression of how a beach could be.

Australia is surrounded by ocean, and we have [one of the longest coastlines of any country in the world](https://www.ga.gov.au/scientific-topics/national-location-information/dimensions/border-lengths). To see that much diversity across ten kilometers was pretty amazing.

Near one bluff, there were several rock pools. There is often used as a nesting ground by [green sea turtles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green_sea_turtle). I don’t think it was the season.

In one rock pool there was a funny-looking leathery thing.

I thought it was a tire.

I looked closer…and it was a turtle!

I’d seen one once before, in the Caribbean waters off the [Yucatán](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yucatán_Peninsula), where I lived a decade ago (which seems insane). That one was further off the coast, swimming along, looking for things.

This one was a meter away, eating green stuff off the rocks.

Because I’m prone to anxiety when I don’t pause, my instinct was: *oh no, what if it’s stuck?*

Don’t turtles have to be submerged all the time?

Turns out they breathe air! This was an enormous shock to discover.

It kept popping its head up every so often, taking an enormous gulp, then carrying on. It holds its breath underwater, the way a dolphin does. I didn’t know that!

I tried to keep a good distance, and it seemed uninterested in my existence, which is always nice.

My panic kept mounting because it had no interest in moving.

I remembered that green sea turtles haul themselves out of the ocean, across the sand, dig a nest, lay those eggs, and haul themselves back in.

Their children do the same after the incubation period.

I thought: I’ll go for a walk. If it still seems unable to get out of the rock pool when I come back, I’ll think about whether anything needs doing.

I’ll admit I can be as complacent as the next person. I didn’t want to be in this instance.

When I came back, it wasn’t there. The tide hadn’t gone out yet, either. It was early morning.

If I’d grabbed the turtle and put it in the ocean, I might’ve thought I was helping. I don’t think I would’ve been. I’d have wasted its time, and making it climb back into the pool to finish breakfast.

The Beetle On Its Back
----------

That’s different again to the beetle.

I was walking a track through a national park, known for its [unique biosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gondwana_Rainforests).

(Yes, we have rainforests. More than one kind, in fact. There’s more to this country than the beach or the stereotypical dust bowl of the Red Centre.)

I can’t remember the beetle’s name, but it was enormous! My hands are small, so I’m not sure that’s an accurate reference, but it was about as long as my middle finger!

It was upside down, waving its legs in the air.

I thought about it. In that instance, the appropriate response (at least I hope it was) seemed to be to grab a leaf, maneuver it upright, and let it go. It shook itself off and continued on its merry way.

I think that one was helpful.

Doing The Dishes
----------

There’s a principle I keep coming back to, which I saw quoted by [Swiss Miss on her excellent online garden](https://www.swiss-miss.com/2026/06/levels-of-agency.html): after dinner, there are people who ask whether they can help, and people who are already washing up. One of those sounds helpful. The other one is.

It’s an interesting way of framing ‘agency,’ and I’m trying to make a conscious effort not to be the person standing around asking whether the dishes need doing when it’s obvious they do.

A bad metaphor for a post about turtles, perhaps. Bear with me.

Because the beetle was a dishes situation, and the turtle was not.

Pausing Is An Action
----------

The world is screaming at you to *do something*, for many valid, important, and essential reasons.

Interacting with any creature: plants, humans, or other animals (I refuse to distinguish humans as their own special category, we’re animals just the same) asks something of you first.

It asks you to be an observer.

A proactive observer, by all means. Not a bystander, if you can help it. But someone attempting to notice when and where their actions would be appreciated.

So: action is important, and pausing before taking that action is also important.

Sometimes observation, or holding space, or listening, is the most important action you can take, even if it feels like doing nothing. In a society, at least the westernized ones, that can feel you’re not doing anything at all.

Every choice is a choice, and every action is an action, regardless of the form it takes.

Paying attention to nature around you, and noticing how it changes through the seasons, is the practice that teaches you the difference.

(An aside, while I’m refusing categories: I’ve thought calling aliens in sci-fi novels *aliens* is strange. Wouldn’t you consider them people? Just a different kind? Wouldn’t we be aliens to them?)

A Coda, Or: Things I Saw
----------

That place was special, so here are a few tidbits.

In the rock pool next to the turtle’s, I saw a [wobbegong](https://australian.museum/learn/animals/fishes/orectolobidae-wobbegongs/), a shark native to the waters around Australia. Compared to a great white it’s tiny, only about a meter long, and it’s a bottom feeder, eating things not unlike what the turtle was eating.

I’d only ever seen them in pictures, in an encyclopedia of Australian marine animals we had at school. The wobbegong stuck in my mind, I think because its name comes from an Indigenous language. Some of the best names for animals and towns in Australia come from Indigenous languages, as unfortunately misinterpreted as they might be by the people who did the naming.

The turtle came back to that same rock pool a few more times while we were there, always around the same hour of the morning. At least I think it was the same: it had the same pattern on its shell. Then I never saw it again.

My time there coincided with [the humpback migration](https://www.nationalparks.nsw.gov.au/get-inspired/stories/whale-migration-the-facts), when they pass along the coast on their way back to warmer water from the Southern Ocean. If you climb a high enough bluff in the early afternoon, you can see them, because the water they expel from their blowholes is extraordinary.

I didn’t know koalas could move any faster than the way you see them move through trees. So you can imagine my surprise, on route to the beach down the main road, to watch one slide down a tree like a firefighter’s pole, streak across the road, and scuttle up a tree on the other side.

I got a photo mid-crossing. No cars were coming, because nobody was on the road at that hour. Three of its four legs are off the ground. It reminded me of [*The Horse in Motion*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Horse_in_Motion).

Kookaburras too, which is always special, because they’re extraordinary.

Plenty of parrots, which I found interesting. I didn’t realize parrots lived so close to seawater, but the mouth of the river wasn’t far, so there was that crossover.

What was one of the most extraordinary things I’ve seen: sitting on the bank of that river, eight hundred meters upstream from the mouth, watching a dolphin teach its child to swim in a circle while flapping its tail, stirring up so much silt that the fish inside couldn’t see their way out.

What the fish do instead is leap, straight into the waiting mouths of the dolphins. [It’s called mud ring feeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mud_ring_feeding), and watching the young one attempt the sequence was…wow.

On a similar note: dolphins doing what looked to me like surfing, on the swell coming into one beach near the river mouth.

The world is extraordinary, when you think about it. Remembering that is the important thing.
