Throughout August I have written a few blog posts about paintings ([example](https://jamesg.blog/2026/08/18/the-hay-wain-by-john-constable)). When I have written these posts, I have [looked closely](https://www.tate.org.uk/art/guide-slow-looking) at a digital or pained photograph of a painting and written my impressions, sometimes with contextual background that I have researched.

For this post, I wanted to try something different. Instead of writing a blog post after I have studied a work, I want you to come along with me as I look at a painting. I am going to write down clues as to what to look out for. For each clue, I want you to spend a few moments – even as much as a minute – looking at the painting. I’ll include details of what I saw, hidden behind a disclosure widget, for you to reference after you have looked. *(Note that you will probably notice things I don’t – indeed, paintings are full of details to appreciate, and part of the joy of studying them is how much there is to see.)*

You don’t need to know anything about art to follow along with this blog post. This is not a formal analysis. We’ll just be talking and thinking about what we see. By the end, I hope you feel at least that looking at art can be fun.

Without further ado, let’s begin.

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

Today we’ll be studying Pierre-August Renoir’s The Skiff (La Yole), painted in 1875 with oil on canvas.

For copyright reasons, I can’t include the photo here, so please [open the National Gallery’s web page](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/pierre-auguste-renoir-the-skiff-la-yole). Their web page has a tool that lets you zoom into the work which you may find helpful for noticing small details. I use the zoom feature often when I am looking at artworks on the web.

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

Before I give any clues to what I see, first I want to ask: what do you see? What stands out to you first? This might be the colour, the landscape, the number of people, where the people are, what pose the people have, or something else.

Spend at least a minute looking at the painting, before then opening my notes.

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

The first thing I notice is the boat in the middle ground, with two people. The lady on the left, in white, is bending slightly forward. It looks almost as if she is mid-row. The oar blends in with the water but is still differentiable. The lady on the left is looking to her right, so we can see her face. The lady on the right is maybe painting forward? Her face and hat are less clear.

</details>

Now, look around. What do you see in the water?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 I noticed the reflection of the boat and the two people in the water. It often takes me a moment to notice reflections in bodies of water depicted in paintings, but when I do I always smile a little bit. What skill it must have taken to paint not only a reflection, but one that is fuzzy and still feels like it fits the object that it represents (in this case, the two people, and the boat).

</details>

Now, look up. What do you see in the sky?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 I noticed what looked like tiny clouds. Their shape was strange. *Are these clouds?* I looked right and noticed there is a bridge. My mind started piecing together the painting. Oh, there's a steam train at the top of the painting!

 But what about the white circle in the top right of the painting? It looks a bit too round to be a puff of smoke from the train. Is it the moon? I don't know. It would be amazing if it was.

</details>

What do you see in the background?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 I first noticed the train bridge on the right hand side. It is quite fuzzy, but the more I look the more I feel like I can see what is in the scene. I wonder: what would it be like to be in that scene? Was the artist standing on the other side of the river? "What makes me think it is a river?" I asked myself. Oh, the bridge.

</details>

Are there other boats?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 It looks like there are two other boats in the background, next to a country house. It looks like there may be a person in the green boat. Looking around the path in the background, I see there are what look like three people: one behind the white garden wall, and two to the right of the wall.

</details>

What else do you see in the water?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 There is the reflection of the house, and the trees to its left. It's amazing that it took so long for me to notice this.

</details>

What do you see on the edges of the artwork?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 The edges of the painting look slightly unfinished. It reminds me of [Berthe Morisot's Woman and Child in a Garden](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/5209), although the edges on that painting are even less finished.

</details>

What season is it?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 There are amber trees on the far left in the background, and the tree behind the boat with the sail is starting to show some amber too. It looks like it could be late summer, maybe autumn?

</details>

Is anything moving?

<details><summary>James' notes</summary>

Although the boat is frozen in the painting -- paintings are frozen in time -- the way the oar moves makes me start to imagine it moving through the water, slowly. I can imagine hearing the water as it moves with the oar, and seeing the ripples of water around.

 Thinking about movement, I realise also that the train in the background is likely moving forward. The water would always be moving too. And the person in the green boat in the background might be preparing to go out on the water. I never used to think much about movement in paintings, but a friend mentioned the idea recently and so I am noticing it more.

</details>

Do you have any questions about the painting?

<details><summary>James' notes</summary>

 Who is the lady who is rowing the boat? Does "skiff" (from the painting title) mean boat? I think so, but I would have to double-check. Did Renoir paint other pictures of boats? Why is the lady's face on the left clearer than the one on the right? You'll likely have your own questions here. You don't need to find the answers. I just love how paintings can make us ask questions.

</details>

How do you feel?

<details><summary>James’ notes</summary>

 My breath has slowed down. I feel calmer. I am delighted by all the colour in the painting. I wonder what details I didn't notice. Why is the bottom right window orange in the painting? Is that because there is a light on? What is that next to the bushed in the bottom left corner? Oh! It's the artist's signature.

</details>

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

I hope you have enjoyed this exercise. I hope that you noticed that spending even a few minutes with a painting is enough to spot many details, and that the more you look, the more you see.

If you found yourself excited by all that you saw, you now know how I feel when I am walking through an art gallery. If you didn’t see the things I pointed out, don’t worry. I spent quite a bit of time looking at this painting, and I have spent a lot of time looking at art in general. If you saw something I didn’t see, great work! There are paintings I have seen several times where I still find new details to appreciate every time I look.

If you want to learn more about this painting, you can read an overview of the work on the [National Gallery website](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/pierre-auguste-renoir-the-skiff-la-yole). There are plenty of fascinating details in the overview.

If you enjoyed this exercise, try searching for a painting on the website of an art gallery near you and see what you see. Or, even better, visit an art gallery in person! If you're not sure where to start in an art gallery. If you know what kind of art you like, go to that area of the gallery and choose a painting that stands out. If you're not sure what kind of art you like, walk around for a bit and, choose one painting, any painting, and look for a few minutes. For reference, today we looked at a French Impressionist painting.
