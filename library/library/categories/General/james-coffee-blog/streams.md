*I wrote this post on Thursday, July 23rd, and reviewed and slightly refined it today. I have left the post as a stream of consciousness – a snippet of my mind as it was on the morning I walked around and saw the world.*

Walking among the stream of people; of stories of life — moving forward and watching other people move forward, of the pace being set by those around you, of feeling comfort being surrounded by others; of living in the contrast of silence and activity — of the journey being the moment between those points; when more stories enter the fore and I realise how much energy I get from watching the world, both at a distance and up close. Can you be distant and present at the same time?

The barista wears a golden badge that reads Director of Happiness, written in a decorative font. Yes, I am now the sort of person who recognises the typography and notes it down — how learning helps us notice what is in front of us; how we learn to recognise; how seeing and recognising are discrete. The barista wore colourful sparkly glasses, and asked how I was to which I shared my perspective on the weather — not too warm. Sometimes we need neutral moments in our conversations; when we can take a moment to think and breathe without needing to go further. I feel the energy of all the people walking in and queuing up, like I did. There is a cycle to the coffee shop — of people, patrons and staff, coming and going, on horizons from the daily to the weekly to the eventual day when new horizons happen.

Someone carries a bouquet of the largest sunflowers I have ever seen. Sir Walter Scott holds an upside down traffic cone between his writers’ arms. People sit on the park benches; one woman takes a corner and reads a book under the shade afforded by a row of trees. Friends ready blankets.

I look down and see an engraving in the pavement. I think immediately back to a deeper engraving on a pavement in a city I love far away — the engraving in my memory and the street is so deep it must have been moulded when the stone was being paved. The moments when we see the passage of time — in the traffic lights and the built up traffic, in the stonework and the glass, in the height and girth of the trees; in the grain of the world.

The moment I walked into the bookshop I smelled the books — stacks of stories waiting for everyone who wants to spend a moment in a new world. I carry dried tears on my right hand from the dust in the air from outside. The tears nonetheless show that I can cry.

I see a book cover showing a building in Central Park, New York, a building that has fascinated me as I have walked around on past trips. Standing taller than the other buildings in its avenue, in the centre of the left side of the garden, the building is a landmark that, when seen, helps you see where you are and how far you have come.

Behind me, a person in the bookstore sings “Do you want a house tour? I’ll take you to the second third floor” to a friend – both are perusing books at the entranceway to the fiction floor. Today I learned I am not the only person who makes music in bookshops — the woman who was singing had a much more melodic voice than I, however. I like humming tunes rather than singing, though: bringing music where there is little. (I wish I could sing well.)

As I walk around I notice that even though I stick to the same track there is always a degree of serendipity — of a particular table standing out that I want to peruse, of a book that mentions the concept of the Anti-Hero in its title that makes me think of Taylor Swift. One day I will understand what a friend was trying to communicate by saying things familiar to us stand out more. Which leads me to wonder: how, in a sea of words, do some things stand out to our minds more than others?

I hear the clinks of cups in the distance. A bookmark reads “be kind to your mind” in a bold pink font with a pink flower and golden sparkles.
