Outside one of my childhood bedrooms there was a tree, a single tree. I see the tree vividly in my mind, and remember tracking it as the seasons changed – watching out for the first blossom, followed by the maturity of the leaves, then again by their browning as summer became autumn and autumn became winter. I saw the seasons while I lived them.

In the moment I didn't think anything of the allure of the tree – why did the tree matter so much to me? Was it because there was only one tree that my attention focused on it? I did look far out toward the hills where there were many trees, but those were harder to see – my impression was characterised by colour and distance; close study was difficult.

As time passes, I study Nature with greater intention: the changes in the seasons, the colours at sunset, the way the fog changes how I see the hills, all fascinate me. I am enamoured by the beauty of Nature.

I love to try to capture what I see in words. Some times, I sit almost resigned to the fact that no word can convey the exact colour of the sunset I am seeing. But therein I see a challenge that excites me: what can I communicate? What do I see? What do I feel?

I never know what to expect when I look around to Nature. This morning my attention was captured by a rabbit walking up a small hill. This evening I see the fog on the hills and wonder how many weeks it has been since I have seen that same fog – a fog that I find so comforting because it is a characteristic of home. As I write, I feel the collective results of my reflections. I can say fog means so much to me because I have spent so much time with fog, looking with wonder.

When I write about Nature, I can be an observer. I can sit with the similarities and differences that I see between hours, days, months, and years. I feel more attuned to the colours of the sky. And in reflecting on what I do know, I realise how much I don’t. Nature’s expanse – and the awe it evokes – invigorates and relaxes me. I don’t need to know everything, or strive to see everything. To look up and around and see what catches my eye, and to ask questions of what I see, is enough to fill an hour, a day. A life?

I see myself in the seasons and feel the seasons impact me. I feel the peaks of summer as I do the depths of winter. I see and feel paces: of growth and change. As I see and feel, I write, atomically, at present. I can report on what I see and discover each day; that I can build a record of the world around me and the lens through which I see it. I write in fascination with how creative works become part of how we understand – of how paintings in museums document times past, and how creative works made now can document the ephemeral present for the forthcoming future.

Herein, I begin to sit with the question “why do I love writing about Nature so much?” I wonder if this is one of these questions that takes a lifetime to answer.
