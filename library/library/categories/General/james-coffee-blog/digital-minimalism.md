*This is my entry for this month’s Bear Blog Carnival, on the topic of* [*staying connected while practicing digital minimalism*](https://juni-mp4.bearblog.dev/bearblog-carnival-june-how-do-you-stay-connected-while-practicing-digital-minimalism-and-how-did-you-make-the-switch/)*. Thank you Juni for hosting the Carnival, and for choosing such a fascinating topic!*

Over the last two years, I have tried to be more intentional about the technology I use. I proactively ask myself the question “do I want to use this tool?” when it comes to websites and applications. When a tool makes me feel overwhelmed, or otherwise doesn’t feel like one I want to use, if the tool provides some meaningful benefit to me or a community I am in, I look for alternatives.

I don’t necessarily strive for digital minimalism, but I do strive to use tools that allow me to be myself online, and allow me to express myself as I want. This means that I have stopped using several tools, like Instagram, that, while being useful at one point, no longer feel like a place I want to spend time.

It takes me a lot of time to leave a digital tool. First, I need to become aware that a tool no longer feels like one I want to use. Second, I consider whether or not to leave. When the time comes when I am ready, I make a plan to move elsewhere. Finally, if a tool is used for communication, I will inform my friends on the platform that I can be found somewhere else.

Leaving tools
----------

In leaving Instagram, for example, I did lose many connections, but I kept the one that I really cared about as we moved over to another communications tool. Indeed, it was my friend who originally said they wanted to spend less time on Instagram and suggested we move. I have great respect for when people say that they would prefer to use another communications medium. As such, I hope that such discussions can become less awkward over time. (Or that we build a network topology such that we can use whatever clients we want to communicate and not have to be locked in to a service run by a huge corporation. <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-1"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#1">1</a></sup>)

I went back to Instagram this year to join another community. I then realised that I couldn’t keep up with everything, and that the application was making me more anxious. I could see the anti-patterns. I could feel the anti-patterns. I had to go. And so I left again, and don’t have any plans on going back.

When I left Discord, I lost many connections, but I was sure to have discussions with friends to see where we could keep chatting. I miss some servers, but I am still able to keep in touch with friends, and I appreciate the communities that existed not only on Discord but elsewhere too such that I can get a pulse of the community even if I’m not in the server. I am a big fan of communities that organise in different places (provided, of course, the facilitators and moderators are fully invested in such an arrangement): it means I can participate even if I’m not in a specific venue. I came back to Discord to join another community, but am set to leave again. I’d much rather start playing around with more tools and see what alternatives are out there.

When I left LinkedIn, I lost touch with many people, but I also spent a few weeks reaching out to people I had worked with to make sure they had my phone number or email address or whatever worked for them. I seldom spoke with people on there anyway, but it was important for me that we could stay in touch. I didn’t want to leave for the sake of leaving: I wanted to leave because the tool didn’t feel good to use. And there was no way I was leaving without telling people I cared about how to contact me.

I don’t try to intentionally reduce my communications mediums as a goal unto itself, as digital minimalism might imply, but rather to use the ones I support and, secondarily, to encourage people to use those tools too. I use Signal over iMessage over WhatsApp. I prefer email for longer-form communication. I use Matrix over Slack over Discord. I still use some tools I don’t love, but being aware of that is enough for me right now: change begins with awareness. (P.S. If you need a Signal friend, [email me](https://jamesg.blog/email) and I’ll share my username!) The effect of preferring some tools over others means that, generally, I am using fewer tools, but, also, there is room for me to experiment with, adopt, and use more tools if one better serves a community I am in.

Services, applications, and newsletters
----------

I do try to reduce how many websites I sign up to, which is important to me for two reasons. First, I don’t want to hold an account with a service I no longer use. Second, I don’t want a service to continue to store my data after I have used said service. I will delete accounts I no longer use.

I also reduce the number of applications I have on my phone. This is important to me because I want my phone to be as calm as possible. I avoid installing apps, and regularly review the applications I have installed and remove ones I no longer need. If I need an application for travel, I will download the application ahead of my trip and delete it afterwards. The fewer icons on my home screen and things I can check, the better.

I keep a few communication apps on my phone such that I can stay in touch with people because that is important to me. With that said, I have notifications turned off on my phone, preferring to manually check chats and applications when I have time. I have my phone in greyscale permanently.

With regard to messaging applications, I leave group chats I am no longer participating in, and stay active in those that I really care about (although I am in some mainly to skim, especially wider group chats for the modules I study at university). Indeed, generally, I stay in communities that I find useful, and contribute where possible. I want to help nurture the communities I care about, even if that means skimming infrequently and answering the occasional question with which I feel I can help.

In addition, I regularly unsubscribe from email newsletters. The calmer my inbox, the happier I am. (NB: Thoughtfully-written reader mail brings me joy.)

Connectedness
----------

Do I feel connected in the way I currently use technology? Yes and no. I speak with many wonderful people every day, both one-on-one in direct messages and in communities. I continue to make and nurture friendships. But I also miss communities I don’t participate in because I don’t use a specific tool. This isn’t down to a desire to be more minimal in how I use technology, but because I am increasingly opinionated on the tools I want to use.

I want to use tools that empower me and the communities I am in, not tools that lock me or us in. And I want others to have that possibility too. It is the discomforting feeling of being locked into a platform – and what that means for people that don’t use said tool – that motivates me to continue running open online events so I can create places for people to connect, to put time into volunteering in communities I care about, and to spend time thinking about how best to organise digital communities.

I offer no advice on whether digital minimalism is good or right for you: our relationships with technology are too varied. Indeed, knowing how hard it is to leave digital spaces makes me acutely aware of how recommending that someone uses fewer tools, or different tools, is a tall order. I still use WhatsApp because I am in communities that organise there and nowhere else, even though I don't want to use the application. Certainly, if I had the chance to influence a change to a different platform in one of those groups, I would speak up for a tool I thought would better suit the group.

With that in mind, I *can* say that you, as a user of technology, have the right to choose what tools you use, and how you use them.
