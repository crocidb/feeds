*V.H. and I are doing a music exchange where we shared three songs with each other and we each chose one to write about. I chose “Lost in Yesterday” by Tame Impala. Due to a curious technical issue – ahem, Apple Music, please design your permalinks more clearly, and make it clear what song they point to when you open the Apple Music interface – “Lost in Yesterday” was not actually an option: V.H. had intended “Breathe Deeper” from the same album to be one of the choices. With that said, we decided to stick with “Lost in Yesterday” as my contribution. This page shows both of our contributions.* [*You can also read this page (with a different preface) on V.H.’s website*](https://vhbelvadi.com/track-exchange-01)*.*

Lost in Yesterday – James
----------

This year I let go of a yesterday. It was always possible to go back, but I decided I wanted to keep moving forward, to keep growing, knowing that I still have the memories of all the smiles and joy; knowing that it’s okay to hold onto a memory, but to choose to move forward, too. In the alternate path, I would have been lost in yesterday.

It was my writing that helped me make the decision. I looked and realised that I had a choice between a past that once made me happy and the choice to try something new. I struggle with change, but change helped me get here, today. I see my writing improving. I feel myself growing. There are so many new experiences to be had. I’d rather be playing guitar right now than being lost in yesterday.

> ‘Cause it hurts to be lost in yesterday

When Tame Impala sing these words, it makes me think about the fine difference between reminiscing and lamenting: one, from joy; the other, from difficulty. I don’t yet know what to say about this other than it is something on my mind. I am in the period of comprehension, on the cusp of understanding. I can see – feel – how it can be so easy to get lost in yesterday.

The song also made me think about the difference between living in yesterday and choosing to stay somewhere, physically. I used to think moving somewhere new was an essential part of growth, but now I realise so much can change in one’s mind without having to physically move. As the days go by, I feel like I see something new even though my surroundings are, on the surface, the same. Here, I’m not lost in yesterday. I’m thinking about the present, and looking forward to the future.

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

When I heard the words “If they hold you, erase them”, something clicked in my mind. I have heard this song before. I don’t remember where, or when, but the muted, electric melody started to sound familiar. It made me think of an experience I had at the Edinburgh Summer Sessions where Zara Larsson sang a song that I didn’t realise she had sung, and one I hadn’t thought about in a while. The song lingered in my mind; so, too, was there a tiny part of this Tame Impala song in my mind.

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

The music video for *Lost in Yesterday* was liminal, showing people at a wedding party getting progressively happier through time, before the tone changes at the end of the video. The [Wikipedia page on the song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_in_Yesterday) helped me understand the video; it was helpful to do a bit of pre-reading as a guide.

In the video, the camera cycled around the room, perhaps alluding to how we can cycle around memories, and how even memories change with time. The liminal vibe makes me think of the feeling of unease that comes from spending too much time with a memory. In particular, it makes me think of how there are some moments I wish I could live in for a moment longer, but, when I think about it, I get to live in this moment, now.

Satisfied Mind – V.H.
----------

I first heard ‘Satisfied mind’ when [Willie Nelson covered it](https://music.apple.com/in/album/satisfied-mind/1443472111?i=1443472229) in 2010 and I have loved the song for its words ever since. What James prompted for me in this exchange was a different cover – from a year earlier – by Bon Iver.

Indeed ‘Satisfied mind’ has been covered by an unusually large number of artists, which I think speaks for how its enduring beauty, its cultural value, its charm and its timelessness are all derived from its words. It is also an incredibly personal song. When Joe Hayes once spoke of this song in an interview, he supposedly said it came from his mother: ‘Everything in the song are things I heard her say over the years.’

That brings me to Bon Iver, or rather to ask *why* Bon Iver? Any review of this song has to appreciate the words, but any review of a song that has been covered in records nearly *fifty* times before should ask what the artist brings to *their* rendition that others have not already done. And in that light Bon Iver is different, if not better, in bringing something quite important: solitude. In a song so beholden to its lyrics it makes both a lot of sense that Vernon chose to sing *a cappella* and little sense that those all-important words were changed ever so slightly – mostly doubled.

What stands out in this track is the Messina effect, <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-1"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#1">1</a></sup> which is unfortunate because Vernon’s voice opens itself, demonstrates a great deal of vulnerability in its draws and pauses, and the electronics overshadows it, needlessly in my opinion. When you do have glimpses of his voice all by itself, you are left wondering what the track might have sounded like had it been truly independent, eg, the first six seconds of the track, or just the word ‘mind’ sung at 3:09 before the music rushes to cover it up seconds later.

Perhaps there is a deeply meaningful sense of juxtaposition at play here. A voice that wants to be vulnerable, yet is fearful of it. And what I assume to be computerised backing vocals add to the sense of enveloping the words as if to say, *I agree* or *Don’t be afraid*. Charmingly, this is peppered throughout the song, but is most obvious when shifting gears, eg, at 0:37 singing ‘so hard to find’ in agreement with the lead vocal, or the ghostly murmur of ‘Is a pauper at times’ around the 1:35 mark. In a sense, all this adds to sections of the lyrics doubling, even for the lead vocal, eg, the doubled lines at 2:04 do not exist in the original <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-2"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#2">2</a></sup>. One would think then that the song itself is longer, so I checked: Bon Iver’s cover is ten seconds *shorter* than Willie Nelson’s. Not having music helps. It also unexpectedly adds to the atmosphere.

This song is lonely at heart and music often gives tracks like this a happier outlook than they perhaps deserve. Music would have made this track feel less lonely, and, ironically, more backing vocals isolate it further. It asks to be talked rather than sung in the way Bon Iver presents it. When viewed through this lens, the computerisation and halation that I first complained about might well work in favour of this track. Their laid-back but rigid presence contrasts against Vernon’s lively, industrious voice to make the song feel looser and more spaced out. And that, in turn, gives you room to think about the truth its words speak.

It is not just Willie Nelson that this track reminds me of but also Bob Dylan. Specifically, Dylan’s *Bootleg Series*, which, like VOLUMES: ONE, is primarily a collection of recordings from concerts. Interestingly, Dylan also seems to have covered ‘A satisfied mind’, but I have never listened to it. There is a thread here I am tempted to pick up but I will resist, choosing instead to keep a *satisfied mind*, recognising the pleasure we have of picking from amongst such great covers, Bon Iver’s being one of the better ones.
