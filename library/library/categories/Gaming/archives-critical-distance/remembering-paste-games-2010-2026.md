Welcome back, readers.

This is a rather impromptu edition of the roundup. It’s not something any of us ever envisioned writing. Indeed, when games media has endured other layoffs and outlet closures we haven’t immediately rushed to memorialize them in this fashion (though maybe we should have done). But when an institution like Paste Games, an outlet that has provided a home to essentially all of our favorite games critics for well over 15 years, is unceremoniously shuttered, it doesn’t feel right to let things go without comment.

Paste has been publishing meaningful games criticism for as long as Critical Distance has been collecting it. In a real sense, Paste set the agenda for good games criticism that we recorded, week in and week out. Paste was a science lab, an incubator, a test area for the kind of thoughtful, incisive criticism we have always wanted to see more of and which more conventional outlets weren’t interested in publishing, and while many people are responsible for this effort, none were so consistently involved and invested in the success of Paste Games as Senior Editor Garrett Martin. It’s thanks to Martin and a revolving team of assistant editors, like Maddy Myers, Gita Jackson, Jenn Frank, Holly Green, Moises Taveras, and most recently, Associate Editor Elijah Gonzalez, that Paste was able to stand as such a indefatigable cornerstone of games criticism through the 2020s. Losing Paste, the house that Martin built, is like waking up to discover that water no longer exists.

There’s a lot one could say about this moment. It’s saddening, naturally; infuriating most definitely. The owners of Paste Media couldn’t see or seemingly understand what kind of tidal force Paste Games had become, and so games criticism as a whole is now poorer for it. Gently, the death of Paste Games, and of games media more broadly, was/is not from natural causes.

Today, though, we’re not here to simply wail and gnash our teeth at the loss of a giant in our space. There is room for that. But there is also room for celebration and reminiscing. And, in the spirit of Steven Santana’s Reading Manifesto, [there is room for reading](https://breakingarrows.substack.com/p/on-culture-missive-november-2025?r=2ieh0). We will keep Paste Games alive by reading, and sharing, and preserving the work everyone who wrote there put out into the world—a body of criticism that was deeply personal, highly political, and often incredibly piercing, that comprises the story of videogames in the modern era.

So, for this roundup, we came up with not one but two lists: articles submitted by Paste writers and our readers; and articles that previously featured in our “This Year in Videogame Blogging” roundups. Astonishingly, there wasn’t as much of an overlap between these two lists as I thought there’d be, and so between the both of them we ended up with an even 140 articles to share. And since this isn’t a typical roundup but a celebration, fuck it—you’re getting ’em all. Quips will be around with the usual later today. I’ll be back in a couple hours with the monthly Patreon roundup. Long live Paste Games! Long live games criticism.

   It feels particularly poignant that the first entry in our list is a letter series between writer Tom Bissell and academic games scholar Simon Ferrari about the nature of games criticism and the tension between storytelling and mechanics both academically and within game development. ### 2014 ###

After a couple of quieter years, Paste returned to TYIVGB with a collection of absolute heaters.

   Cara Ellison contends with the representational problems around *LA Noire*, the way the exclusion of women and people of color has been normalized not just in games but in film and other media, and considers some fairly commonsense solutions to the issue.

   Samantha Allen works through the physical strangeness of explaining how to drift in *Mario Kart 8* to a friend.
* **[Kentucky Route Zero’s Musical Centerpiece and the Power of Choice | Ansh Patel](https://www.avclub.com/kentucky-route-zeros-musical-centerpiece)**  
   Ansh Patel *Zero*es in on “Too Late to Love You” and the role participatory theatre plays in *Kentucky Route Zero*‘s mysterious narrative.
* [**Working For the Love of the Game: The Problem With Blizzard’s Recruitment Video | Ian Williams, Austin Walker**](https://www.avclub.com/working-for-the-love-of-the-game-the-problems-with)  
   Ian Williams and Austin Walker confront the false solidarity of a Blizzard recruitment tape that claims the company’s workers as “family” while keeping them open to familiar workplace turbulence (and as we’d find out, abuses).
* [**Femme Doms of Videogames: Bayonetta Doesn’t Care If She’s Not Your Kink | Maddy Myers**](https://www.avclub.com/femme-doms-of-videogames-bayonetta-doesnt-care-if)  
   Maddy Myers challenges games’—and games criticism’s—continued insistence on framing depictions of women owning their sexuality in terms of the “male gaze” (**Further Reading**: [Maddy’s eulogy for the queer, empowered Bayonetta that should have been and maybe never was](https://www.mothership.blog/rest-in-peace-bayonetta/)).
* [**Black Steel In The Hour of Chaos: Assassin’s Creed and the Power of Representation | Justin Clark**](https://web.archive.org/web/20150117010418/http://www.pastemagazine.com/articles/2014/11/black-steel-in-the-hour-of-chaos-assassins-creed-a.html)

* [**The Crew: Postcard America | Austin Walker**](https://www.avclub.com/the-crew-review-postcard-america)  
   Austin Walker finds the pure joys of driving through America too adulterated by Ubisoft’s insistence on including as much bizarre, often conflicting, power fantasy nonsense in *a game about driving* as possible.

>
>
> *The Crew* is not *Eurotruck Simulator* for the United States. Even though they’ve meticulously crafted this sprawling space, they don’t seem to understand that there’s joy in these mundane places. That’s where the magic is, but *The Crew* thinks it’s a better idea to offer wide shots of fireworks and stadiums and bridges like a poor man’s Michael Bay film. But it’s not the second coming of *Burnout Paradise*either. It’s too staid for that. It’s just a clipboard and a list.

>
>
> Queer existence isn’t defined by suffering, though, and Kainé and Emil’s lives are no different. Backmatter
----------

This of course is not a complete archive of what got published to Paste Games over the years. There’s a little bit of recency bias when it comes to which eras and people are represented, and we suspect the list will grow as people make more suggestions. This memorial will serve as a living document to what Paste Games meant to our community. But what strikes me, looking at everything from a bird’s eye view, is how similar the conversations we’re having in the mid-2020s seem to the ones being had in 2011, 2014, 2019.

We can draw a throughline between what we were writing about—what we cared about—over a decade ago, and what we care about today, and I think that’s something special. But it shouldn’t take the death or restructuring of an outlet, or the layoffs of a bunch of incredibly talented writers, to bring us this kind of revelation. Bringing it back to Santana’s Reading Manifesto, “reading fellow critics works enriches not only our lives but our ability to articulate ideas, to refine rough thoughts, to be introduced to perspectives, ideas, and thoughts not previously considered because we simply could not look beyond ourselves without another speaking to us from outside.”

These roundups exist to help us stay sharp, to be able to take a longer view of the critical landscape and progress arguments and ideas forward, rather than stay stuck in the same circular debates over the same few items, over and over and over again. My hope is, you’ll read the articles linked above, take inspiration from them, and find new avenues of criticism to pursue. In so doing, we can keep the spirit of Paste Games alive.
