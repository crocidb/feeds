### 1. Introduction ###

I only really engage with relatively deep articles nowadays which fall under an RSS feed, and provide a Webmention end-point for feedback. I have recently started to acknowledge my appreciation for these sites directly.

Webmentions<sup><a href="#webmention">[1]</a></sup> form the back-bone of the Indieweb<sup><a href="#indieweb">[2]</a></sup>: a truly distributed flavour of social media. Here, everybody runs their own server (okay, this is generally not true, but is the spirit of it). And that means that everybody owns their own data, their postings to this social medium. And that includes comments on othersʼ articles. And Webmentions are the means by which such comments are attached to their respective article.

People should use this as it gives them more control over what material is associated with their site, and establishes webs of like-minded discourse.

However, there is a lot of umming and arring in the microblogging space, particularly the *fediverse*, where there is some disagreement that personal blogs should be regarded at all as social media discussion centres. It is fine by me for you to do whatever you want to do with your own web site—that is the point of having it after all—but I find the consumption of blogged content more satisfactory if I can either reinforce the message or explain my issues with it. Just writing a response on my own blog and making a one-way link to the original article doesnʼt connect the response properly, in my opinion.

### 2. The Statistics ###

In my estimation one in every three blogs supports Webmentions to some degree. Specifically I counted 22 out of 63 blogs which I follow (via RSS), which equates to 35% of all the sites.

Admittedly some of these are abandonned, and quite a few ‘accept’ webmentions but donʼt actually do anything about them (I suspect they are simply subscribed to *webmention.io* and advertise the fact, but donʼt actually pull the data back through the API).
