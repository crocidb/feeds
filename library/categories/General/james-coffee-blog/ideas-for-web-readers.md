Every so often I have an idea for a feature that might be interesting in a [web reader](https://jamesg.blog/2025/03/21/web-readers-and-rss-readers). I write down my ideas in my notes. Sometimes, an idea makes its way into Artemis, the calm web reader I maintain. Other times, the idea stays in my notes where it can breathe, mature, and maybe become something (or maybe not; choosing what not to build is just as important as choosing what to build).

I wanted to write down a few of the ideas I have had for web readers. Not all of these may be interesting, but I thought I’d get them on (digital) paper anyway.

A weekly boost round-up
----------

Artemis allows users to subscribe to accounts on Mastodon. When this feature is used, Artemis only displays posts authored by the person a user has chosen to follow (which also includes replies to the author’s posts written by the author, aka a thread). This is because Artemis assumes you want to read posts by the author; boosts can clutter the reader interface.

This made me think that there could be a page in a web reader that lets you see posts your friends have bookmarked or boosted once per week. The idea is that this may be a calmer way of exploring posts people you follow are sharing. [Artemis already has a “roll-up” feature](https://jamesg.blog/2026/01/19/announcing-artemis-roll-up) that lets you see posts from an author once per week. A refinement on this idea is a roll-up that specifically shows “boosts” like shares of posts on Mastodon.

Calendar view
----------

Last year I experimented with adding calendar subscriptions to Artemis. The idea was that you could follow calendars in your reader.

I never did much with this idea, but I do wonder the extent to which calendars could fit in with web readers. I thought it would be cool if the web reader told you it was a bank holiday at the top of every bank holiday, which could be retrieved here in the UK by following the government’s bank holidays ICS file.

It might be the case that calendaring and web readers should be entirely separate, but the idea is still in my mind nonetheless. Of note, I don’t think a web reader should be a primary calendar: rather, a calendar view would let you see dated events in your reader.

Another idea is that it could be interesting for a reader to show a message saying you shouldn’t be looking at your reader if you have a calendar event scheduled. <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-1"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#1">1</a></sup>

Plain English algorithm explanations
----------

Artemis displays posts from authors users follow in a reverse chronological list of posts by default (the other options are alphabetical by title or post author). This means that everyone can readily answer the question *Why am I seeing this?* with “because I chose to follow this person.” I believe it is essential that people should be able to confidently describe why they are seeing something when using Artemis. I don’t like opaque algorithms.

Users can now add filters to their feed, which means that the answer to *Why am I seeing this?* may be more nuanced. For example, a user may see a post because it was published by an author they follow *and* because it contains a keyword. This got me thinking about what a plain English algorithm explanation would look like. For example, Artemis could say:

> How your reader works  
>   
> Your reader displays posts authored by people you have subscribed to in Artemis.  
>   
> Your reader filters out all posts that contain the word “meow.” [change/remove filter]  
>   
> Your reader also filters out posts by John Appleseed that contain the word “pear” [change/remove filter]  
>   
> Your reader displays the last seven days of posts.

All of this information is already available in the system because the user has configured these filters. The idea here is to provide a plain-text description. This description would have to be carefully written to be both understandable and concise. Concision is especially important if someone follows dozens of authors and has dozens of filters set up.

Maybe this idea doesn’t scale to lots of filters, or maybe it does; maybe the description could say “Your reader filters out all posts that contain one of 12 keyword filters you have set. [see keyword filters]”with a `details` HTML tag that opens to show all the keywords.

Text-based interface
----------

Another idea I had was a single text input field in Artemis that could be used to do tasks like follow an author, add a keyword filter, or start a search query. This is inspired by the [Chrome Dev Tools command bar](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/devtools/command-menu). The text field could accept inputs like:

* A URL, which would prompt an option to show up in a dropdown asking if the user wants to subscribe (or notifying the user they have already subscribed to the feed if the URL is a feed).
* “filter [keyword]” to create a keyword filter that applies to all users.
* “filter [keyword] on [feed]” to create a keyword filter that applies to a specific author.
* Any other text, which would ask the user if they want to submit it as a query to search posts or search for a specific author.

This would exist as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, visual interfaces. The text-based interface would be for advanced users who are comfortable working with a text-based action bar.

Image mode
----------

I briefly experimented with an image mode for Artemis. An image mode would allow you to see new images once per day, just as Artemis updates the list of posts in your reader once per day. This would offer a calmer experience than social networks where images can be available at any time in a feed.

A image grid interface similar to that of [some.pics](https://some.pics/) but that prioritises the title or caption of a post could be interesting. It would also be cool if there was an option to visually display any alt text associated with an image in the reader interface.

I tried experimenting with an image mode by showing images associated with a post directly below post titles in Artemis (separate from the main reader) but it didn’t feel like the feature worked as implemented: the single column layout meant that there was a lot of open space on the page that wasn’t being used. Indeed, Artemis was designed for text – a different interface would have to be envisioned specifically for the medium of images.

By extension, a reader for Stories could be interesting. This could be done using the [Open Stories](https://github.com/dddddddddzzzz/OpenStories) format and, potentially, the [accompanying open source custom element for viewing stories](https://github.com/dddddddddzzzz/open-stories-element). I would still like to see this done where you could opt in to only seeing posts once per day.

From an engineering perspective, in all cases, the reader should automatically strip EXIF data, proxy external URLs so a site author cannot track if/when someone has viewed a post, and be implemented in such a way that it uses as little data as possible on weaker mobile connections. A “low data mode” for an image reader would be an excellent feature.

I have another idea too: what about an image viewer that *only* shows alt text?

Bonus: Add an icon if you support a blog
----------

When I open The Guardian, I see a banner that says "Thank you Your support powers our independent journalism".

What if a web reader let you say that you have supported an author with a financial contribution which leads to an icon appearing next to their name when they publish a post? This could be as simple as a checkbox setting that you could toggle. Then, as you visit your reader, you'll have a little reminder that you're actively supporting blogs and publications you like. <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-2"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#2">2</a></sup>

Bonus #2: Customisable navigation bar and CSS
----------

What if a web reader let you add a customisable link to the navigation bar? The link could take you to your personal website, for example, or whatever other website you want to be easily accessible from your reader. This small detail would let people make their reader their own a tiny bit more.

Also related to this idea is that a reader could let someone specify custom CSS so they can fully customise the UI. There are likely numerous security considerations to take into account, but this feature would let people make custom themes that they could share with others. [Bear Blog has a rich community of people making themes](https://grizzlygazette.bearblog.dev/introducing-the-first-custom-bearblog-theme-repository/), with CSS as the means by which themes are written, shared, and used.

Conclusion
----------

These are some of my ideas for web readers that I have had recently. Whether I get around to implementing any of these, I am not sure, This post is not a roadmap, rather a list of some ideas that I have had. With that said, I wanted to share the ideas in case they inspire other people to make things.
