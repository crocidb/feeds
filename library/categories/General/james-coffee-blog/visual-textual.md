My home page is a poem. I designed it in this way to explore the extent to which a literary medium – a poem – could be used for navigation, for [way finding](https://jamesg.blog/2026/08/10/poems-as-wayfinding). Poems help me see the world differently, to navigate thoughts. So too does navigation: every link is a bridge to a new place, to new thoughts. While the style is unconventional and less clear than traditional navigation bars, I love it. My home page brings me closer to poetry, and reminds me of my love of words.

For the last few weeks, I have had an idea in the back of my mind: what if my home page poem had a few icons in the background that served as visual cues to different stanzas in the poem? These icons might make it easier to find particular sections of the poem. The would also add a more visual dimension to the poem. While poems are visual in themselves – the spaces between words and stanzas, indentations, the lengths of lines, the space the poem takes on a page, the typography used, and the symbols used, among other factors, all influence how we read a poem – images add a new dimension.

One of the things I love most about the web is that I can ask myself “what would this look like?” and then, through code and tinkering, bring the idea to life. I spent some time this morning searching through [Phosphor Icons](https://phosphoricons.com/), an MIT-licensed icon set I use for, among other things, the icons in my mobile navigation bar. I decided on a flower for the first stanza, which speaks of stories and poems and ideas and joy, a notebook for the second, where my most recent blog posts are listed, and footsteps for the third stanza, which is more about navigation. I am not fully set on footsteps for the third stanza, as they don’t quite relate to the invitation to say hello at the beginning of the stanza. Indeed, part of the creative challenge of this concept is to find an image that matches many words.

Here is what I designed:

![](https://jamesg.blog/assets/uploads/iconhomepage.png)

Faint blue icons appear on alternating sides of each stanza: right, left, right. The icons are all at slight angles. An opacity is applied so that the icons do not stand out as much as the words in the poem. The icons complement the text in each stanza to varying extents.

Thinking about the idea of adding images to a poem brought me back to a list I started curating in the last month of places where words exist next to images. Words appear on album covers as part of the art designed to represent the album. Words appear in front of or near images on book covers. In museums, words appear on some paintings (i.e. signatures, or even, in some cases, stories), next to art (i.e. on information plaques), below art (i.e. on a label on the frame of a painting), and more. Words and images, although separate modalities, appear together so often. Indeed, I have started to add more images to my home page that was originally all-text except for my website mascot; the images I have since added – two buttons – convey information in a way I can’t with words, but also serve as a complement to what is on the page.

While I haven’t rolled out this design on my home page – and I’m not sure yet if I will – it has been interesting to explore the intersection of the visual and the textual in this way. Indeed, my poem also explores a third dimension – navigation – through the links it uses. It is fascinating to try and extend a literary medium with the affordances that hypertext allows. What if a poem revealed a piece of art every time you hovered over a stanza?

My main reservation with the icon design is that it may distract from the words. But it also creates new visual anchors. There is a lot to think about.

This crossing of disciplines relates to a conversation I had earlier this year with an uncle when I mentioned that I don’t think the sciences and the arts are disconnected, but rather are complementary. I can’t remember his exact words, but I was left feeling excited about how someone else also didn’t see them as separate buckets. Similarly, it is interesting to think of words and images not only in the context of each modality, but in the context of all the spaces in between. Indeed, we already have picture books, prose poetry, technical writing, visual communication, and many other forms that combine two concepts or traditions together.
