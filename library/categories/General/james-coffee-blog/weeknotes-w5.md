It is hard to believe it is weeknotes time again. This week has *flown* by. We had our first spell of cooler weather; winter is getting closer. The morning frost has been pretty. It is not yet cold enough to have seen my breath while out on a walk, though. I am hoping that with the cooler weather I will take my weekend mornings slower and read a little bit more. I started reading a new fiction book this weekend. I felt so proud when I looked at the book after a day of reading and found I was over a third of the way through.

The highlights
----------

I completed my first art history assignment of this term! I was nervous about this assignment because it was a step up from my previous assignments last year, but I tried my best and am happy with what I wrote. The assignment had two parts and, this week, I ended up doing *four drafts* of the second part. The task involved comparing two art works and, on my first three attempts, I found pieces that I later realised did not meet the assignment criteria. Rather than being discouraged, I actually quite enjoyed how engaged I ended up being with more artwork than I expected. With that said, I am glad that the fourth time was the charm.

I am feeling a lot more confident about the rest of the year in my art history classes now that I have worked on my first assignment. Next, we are moving on to *Art in the making*, an examination of different mediums of artworks and, I think, materials used. We are starting with learning about sculpture, for which I am excited, and will then be going through paintings, and other media. This will take me up to the Christmas break, after which point there will be a little bit more in the block before our second assignment.

Also, as I mentioned earlier, I started reading a fiction book this weekend: *Yellowface*, by Rebecca F. Kuang. This book is unlike any other I have read, and I love how the narrator seems relatively unreliable. I am about a third of the way in, and excited to keep making my way through. Most excitingly, this book has been the first fiction book that I have made this much progress on in several months. I hope this is the start of a little bit more (non-academic) reading. With that in mind, I do want to make progress on *The Story of Art*, which, while not part of my studies, is something I want to keep reading. But I think I’ll prioritise reading fiction in my free time for now.

[Writing Month](https://jamesg.blog/writing-month-2026) has been continuing to go well. I have written at least one haiku every day over the last week. On many days, I have written more than one, or several. Although haikus are written in so few words, I still feel like I have much to learn. Every time I set out to write one, I feel challenged. I like this feeling. I thought I might write more longer poems this month, but haiku seems to be the format into which I have settled. If you, dear reader, have any tips on how to write haikus, or any guidance to which you could point me, please don’t hesitate to [send me an email](https://jamesg.blog/email).

I have been attending a lot of online events over the last few weeks related to the web, writing, art, and more, in addition to my university lectures. Next week is scheduled to be a little bit quieter, for which I am grateful. While I love online events, I also feel like I have been on a lot of calls lately, so having a break will be nice.

On instrumentalisation
----------

*This note turned out to be long enough that I broke it out of the “Thoughts, wandering” section of the weeknote into its own section.*

Earlier this year, I read an essay that introduced me to the idea of “instrumentalisation”. Published in Aeon, the [essay defines instrumentalisation as follows](https://aeon.co/essays/instrumentalisation-is-making-everything-a-means-to-an-end):

>
>
> For a long time, I have been privately lamenting the instrumentalisation of everything: how nothing seems to be of value in itself anymore but is only seen as useful in the service of some utilitarian function.
>
>

The first three paragraphs of the essay are a great start to learn about the concept, where the author talks about the [UK National Art Pass and marketing art](https://www.artfund.org/our-purpose/news/art-fund-promotes-the-health-benefits-of-art-in-its-autumn-campaign) around how it can help our health. There is evidence that art can help our health. [Daisy Fancourt’s book *Art Cure*](https://www.penguin.co.uk/books/462340/art-cure-by-fancourt-daisy/9781804950784) is an *excellent*, well-researched, and, generally, wonderful, read on the way art can influence our health. With that said, what interests me most regarding instrumentalisation is the idea that one might market art around what it can do for us.

On Friday, I went to the first day of an online conference about [education and writing](https://www.nawe.co.uk/writing-in-education/nawe-conference.html). I wasn’t really in the target audience, but I did enjoy the sessions I attended. The keynote speaker mentioned the word “instrumentalisation”, and suddenly I was connecting dots between the concept and writing.

This brings me to [Olu’s recent blog post](https://olu.online/whats-the-point/) which invites readers to contemplate their personal motivations for writing. I wanted to bring the concept of “instrumentalisation” into this discussion. I have heard a lot about how writing can help you improve your thinking, and many other such discussions over the years. But, at least right now, for me the main motivation for writing is that it is a true joy. I enjoy writing. I feel like I am pushing myself. I feel myself getting better and, all the while, I feel there are so many places I could learn.

One of my favourite quotes from my favourite movie, Tomorrowland, is:

>
>
> Nix: How would your jet pack make the world a better place? Young Frank Walker: Can't it just be fun?
>
>

I think this applies, too, in terms of writing and blogging. Perhaps this is another way of saying “do art for art’s sake.” This argument, however, I think can be a little bit abstract. What does it mean to do something for art’s sake? Perhaps the better articulation is to do something for play. But, I’d much rather say do something because you like it.

When it comes to new hobbies, I am not appealed by drawing because it might help me achieve some other goal than to get better at drawing. Equally, I appreciate all the research that is going into how art can help us, and am fascinated by the work Fancourt and so many other researchers are doing on what art generally can help us achieve. The National Art Pass’ campaign, if I had seen it in person, may have even illicit excitement in me. *Art can make us feel better?* Again, I defer to Fancourt’s book on the myriad ways that art can impact us, in so many settings.

That is all to say that, amid all of the possible reasons to do something, perhaps the one we should speak about more is that we do it because we love it. I maintain this website in part because I build new skills but, more importantly, I do it because I love it. And I think, especially in this era of such technological change, I’d might be hard pressed to keep going with making a website without seeing and feeling something uniquely interesting about websites that brings me to them day in, and day out. Equally, I am enjoying making zines in part because I can make something in a medium that is intentionally focused on smaller groups of people. I have been loving making zines that are only available to friends. Zines are amazing.

I tried to do something in the middle where I used my fine-liners – that I bought surely over two years ago and have almost never used – to make some colourful patterns, and to do something like “sketch notes” alongside a talk I attended this week. It was quite relaxing to draw along to what I was listening to, even if most of my “drawings” were either patterns or words. Maybe I should play around with drawing lines and see where I go, rather than rushing into trying to draw faces, which I have tried in the past but have not kept up with.

Miscellanea
----------

* “[Ways of Not Seeing](https://www.bbc.co.uk/sounds/play/m002kf96)”, an episode of the BBC Radio 4 Artworks show, was a *terrific* listen. It talks about different ways that audio descriptions could be done for paintings. I found this via [Brett’s excellent “Audio Description in Art and Museums”](https://brett.coulstock.id.au/audio-description-in-art-and-museums.html) list of resources. I will note by way of a content warning that the “Ways of Not Seeing” episode talks about a painting with nudity.
* “[Are thirst traps the ultimate self-portrait?](https://plastermagazine.com/opinion/thirst-traps-ultimate-self-portrait/)” (via the [S.C. 2.4.4 newsletter](https://buttondown.com/ericwbailey/archive/sc-244-these-techniques-shouldnt-be-proprietary/)) was an interesting read. I am fascinated by ways that we choose to express and represent ourselves.
* I really want to try a [Croffle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croffle).
* The article “[How much art does your council own and is it locked away or on display?](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/crgqwgzxeexno)” led [V.H.](https://vhbelvadi.com) and I down several rabbit holes. This article deserves to have more data visualisations than the single table in which all of the data is presented. I would love a map view of the data. I also think this article leads to many more questions. Why are some works not displayed? Why do some councils have more artworks than others? What definitions was every local authority using for art?
  * One stat that stood out was the huge collection owned by Waltham Forest. I looked into it and Waltham Forest has 125,000 pieces because of, I think, the [Vestry House Museum](https://vestryhousemuseum.org.uk/collection/), which “holds over 100,000 objects relating to the history of the London Borough of Waltham Forest.” [This includes](https://vestryhousemuseum.org.uk/collection/photographic-collection/) “comprises approximately 80,000 photographic prints, negatives, lantern slides and postcards dating from the 1860s onwards”.

* I enjoyed reading Joe’s blog post “[Aura, Spirit](https://artlung.com/blog/2026/11/10/aura-spirit/).” I would love to read more perspectives on the concept of aura. Tracy’s work in this area is, in my mind, verging on canonical in terms of [aura and digital media](https://tracydurnell.com/2025/12/14/the-signifiers-of-digital-aura/). I highly recommend her writings on the topic.
* I finished watching a romance anime I have been enjoying lately. Looking for something new, I watched the first episode of “[Reborn as a Vending Machine, I Now Wander the Dungeon](https://www.crunchyroll.com/series/GVDHX8504/reborn-as-a-vending-machine-i-now-wander-the-dungeon)” this week. The concept is *wild*. The first episode was *okay*, but I don’t plan to watch another one. I have settled on another romance anime next, which I am enjoying.
* [This perspective by @[email protected] on software](https://friend.camp/@aparrish/117593683064513260) is *fascinating*, specifically “but that students are culturally predisposed to a belief that they're not smart enough to program” (emphasis removed). I do wonder how many people feel like having a website isn’t for them because it sounds too complicated. I also wonder how many people are put off by HTML because it looks too complicated. Especially now, it feels more pertinent than ever to do all we can to show that HTML, CSS, the web itself, is *for everyone*. Tim Berners-Lee said it himself in the title of his most recent book, [This is For Everyone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/This_Is_for_Everyone). This topic deserves a lot of exploration, more than I have space for in this weeknote – and I think I need to reflect more on the idea before I hold any opinion – but I did want to include it here because it feels appropriate to constantly re-examine complexity and its perceptions in a technical field like web development. (Even the very fact that I said “technical field” first says something (I haven’t edited it so I can make this point). *Words matter.*)
