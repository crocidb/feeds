Yesterday I asked three friends a variation of the following question:

>
>
> Is there a website that makes you pause like a piece of architecture?
>
>

One person provided an answer to the question that was a hypothetical design: a website where, when you scrolled down, you would be moved forward through the archives, with representations of pages to your left and right. Two people – and, additionally, myself – couldn’t think of an answer. <sup class="footnote-reference" id="f-1"><a href="https://jamesg.blog/longform-feed#1">1</a></sup>

I was motivated by the way that tall buildings in particular make us look up. Architecture makes us feel a certain way (as do websites). There are depths of detail to be realised. A building can be examined from different perspectives, both physically, and intellectually in terms of history and use and style and more.

While there may not be an answer to this question, I am fascinated by trying to bring what I learn about art into the context of websites. Indeed, websites are part of our visual culture. Site authors make decisions about how a site is designed. Even a page that is relatively minimal can be studied; the typography, text sizes, navigation bar design, use (or non-use) of images, are all visual artefacts that contribute toward the design, feeling, and use of a website.

Along these same lines, websites are an artistic medium unto themselves, blending all the potential of visual and textual communication, information architecture, hypertext, and more. (As an aside, it feels like many mediums end up being a combination of mediums: old manuscripts commonly contained illustrations (i.e. the Book of Kells), paintings can contain text (i.e. signatures, names) or stamps (such as those used in Chinese art to designate ownership), magazines contain both images and text, and so on.)

The aforementioned discussion led me to wonder about a few different things.

* Is it harder for a web page to make you pause with curiosity in the same way that monumental architecture does? Or, generally, is it hard for a web page to make you pause?
* What would a website homepage look like that was literally a map?
* What does “patina” mean in the context of websites?

I also had an idea for a web page that describes the visual design of my website, as if offering alt text for the complete visual experience on a page. I have already written about the [design process for the current version of this website](https://jamesg.blog/2024/06/28/new-design) before, but I haven’t written a concise representation of the exact visual experience.

There are likely many other questions that could be asked that exist at the intersection of websites and art, and art history. I look forward to continuing to think about this in my studies.
