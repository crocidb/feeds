In 2024 I [started writing monostiches](https://jamesg.blog/2024/06/01/the-joy-of-the-monostich), which are poems that span a single line. Over the last few weeks I have been writing more, and now I have a [category page devoted to monostiches](https://jamesg.blog/monostich).

I have been thinking about why I like the monostich form so much. I think there are a few reasons. First, monostiches are short. On days where committing to writing a longer poem can be daunting, writing a single line feels more tractable. This doesn’t mean that monostiches are necessarily easy. I write monostiches to evoke a particular feeling, or to express something that I have felt. Thus, what I write, as with all of my poetry, needs to feel true to me.

Second, I have found monostiches are a playground for language. One format I have used in the past is “the {x} of {y}”, like “the weight of moonlight”. This week I thought “what word could I use instead of ‘the’?” which led me to researching what kind of word “the” is. I learned “the”, like “a”, is an “article.” Semantics aside, when I write a monostich I scrutinise what I am writing to see if it evokes what I want. In this way I can feel the edges of a word – what does a word mean, does it apply in the way I want to use it, what ambiguities can I embrace, what ambiguities are so unclear that they are unlikely to be useful?

I have also been thinking about monostiches as both a way to document feelings, and a way to experiment with different subject matters. Here is one monostich I wrote recently:

>
>
> The surreality of the past.
>
>

I wrote this while on a train going through the countryside here in Scotland. I was thinking back to memories of past trips and how some of them feel surreal. There was something both delightful and surprising about the fidelity with which I could recall a particular coffee shop I enjoyed, for example. This monostich has a certain aura I haven’t embraced in my writing (dark academia esque?). It was nice to write these words to capture that aura as a potential place of exploration.

Importantly, many of my monostiches do not graduate into a longer poem, or a longer piece of writing. I like how a monostich can stand on its own, evoking feelings and provoking thought without having to be longer. Indeed, figuring out how many different feelings I can evoke with only a handful of words is an exciting challenge. Here is another one I wrote recently:

>
>
> Where there is, there was; where there was, there is.
>
>

I also wrote this one on a train. Thinking back, perhaps the environment in which I wrote the poem subconsciously influenced me, for, as I sat down, looking out the clear window to my right, everything was moving: time and what I saw moved from the present to the past. I thought about this arrangement of words – a fragment of a thought – as a means of considering how places carry the residue of the past. Everywhere there is something, there was also something. Where there is a tree, there may have been nothing; where there is nothing, there may one day be a tree.

I wrote “[ever-changing](https://jamesg.blog/2026/09/30/ever-changing)” today to experiment with incomplete sentences. Taylor Swift’s new song “Cleveland” contains two lines that seem to suggest there is a missing word. I wondered if I could do something similar. “ever-changing” reads:

>
>
> My website is ever changing. I am
>
>

When I read this, my mind says “My website is ever changing. I am ever changing.” Perhaps this is because I have spent so much time with the words, but there is something interesting about the second sentence provoking more words: a poem that writes itself.

One thing I have been thinking about is whether I should write commentary to accompany my monostiches, like I have done above, to document the thought that went into them: what my intent was, what the words mean to me, and what different interpretations I had come up with. For instance, I have this monostich in my notes:

>
>
> The weight of Time’s artefacts.
>
>

There are a few questions that arise from this monostich:

* What are time’s artefacts?
* What if time was heavy? (Is time heavy?)
* Why is Time capitalised? (What does it mean for time to be personified?)

I’m not sure if these are immediately obvious from reading the work, but they all did come to mind.

In terms of why I write monostiches, first and foremost I love writing them. Second, they feel like a place to practice, to sit with words, to think about ideas, and to embrace the ambiguity and multiple meanings that we can find in language and that we can imbue in poetry and prose. Monostiches remind me of how evocative words can be. And they also push me to think about a way of writing that I don’t as of yet bring into my prose. I am unsure how I would fit “The weight of Time’s artefacts.” into a longer piece, but the words still mean something to me.

Maybe one day my monostiches will inspire longer pieces, but for now I love the challenge of writing single lines with multiple meanings. Indeed, perhaps monostiches reflect something I have been feeling recently: some ideas need time to develop. Writing – getting anything on the page – helps to propel me forward.
