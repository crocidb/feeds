I was listening to Lorde’s *Melodrama*, an album I haven’t listened to for some time. Her music continues to echo through my week. It is hard to believe that I got to see her perform live a few days ago; the electricity of the performance still sticks with me: the moments we all sung with all the passion we had.

*Do I listen to the same song over again because I want to feel it more?* I thought about this while sitting on a bench looking out onto green bushes and idly watching as the world went by.

My collective experiences of listening to music span all the moods of which I can think: joy, sadness, passion, confusion, blankness, affinity with the environment, and so much more. Sometimes I listen to music because I want to hear something; to feel something. Other times I listen to music to pass the time. Or to have something that might make me smile. Music can bring life to my day, or make the day easier; music can bring more energy than I can describe to a moment, like Lorde’s performance did earlier this week.

Every song and album fuels me in a different way. I listen to Bon Iver’s piano performance when I want to feel every note I can. I have listened to his Volumes One to help me fall asleep. I listen to Taylor Swift’s *Would’ve Could’ve Should’ve* when I want to sing passionately. I listen to cardigan when I want to feel cosy. I listen to Misterwives’ *Nosebleeds: Encore* on the bus, even though it doesn’t really fit with the vibe of looking out a window to the moving countryside.

I listen to The Colourist when I want to feel more energy as I move around a place; their music is the kind that gives me a spring in my step. I have a vivid memory of a particular sunny morning on which their music brought so much joy.

I am listening to Paris Paloma’s music a lot lately because I want to explore the potential intersections of music and art history in greater depth. I listen to Oh Wonder’s “No One Else Can Wear Your Crown” when I do a particular walk through Edinburgh. It’s tradition to listen to the album on that walk at this point. The music brings me comfort.

Songs and albums become soundtracks for journeys, for different moments. They become pillows I can carry with me everywhere on a hard day. They become sparks that enliven my mind, and make me want to sing. Occasionally, music makes me wonder “maybe I can play this song, too.” And then I will go to my piano or guitar and try my best to play what I can, following chord charts for guitar or my ear for piano.

Time adds a new dimension to music: the more I listen, I make more memories, I notice more details, I learn more about what a song means to me. The more I listen to a song, the more confident I grow in beginning to play the song by ear.

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

I remember where I first played Taylor Swift's "The Bolter". It was on a train station piano after a long trip. I had listened to the song several times and I was eager to find any piano on which to play. I don't usually play songs for the first time in public, but I had to make an exception for this song. I was so excited to play it.

I can't remember exactly how well I played The Bolter, but I do remember the joy of being back at a piano, ready to play again, and of being proud at how well it went. All the time I spent listening to the album prepared me to play (albeit approximately, but all of my performances are approximations; the fact that every time I play a song is unique is one of the things I enjoy most about playing by ear).

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

This week Taylor Swift released a video of her performance as part of [The Icon Sessions at the Grammy Museum](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_9jaJtmraXA), a mashup of three songs. The video brings me back to the Eras Tour: the floral piano, the flower on her dress. (Will she release an acoustic album of mashups one day? I really hope so!)

Even though I know all the songs, the performance is new. The points that Swift accentuates are different. My familiarity with the songs forms the base from which my ear can start to play along – to become part of a performance that took place thousands of miles away, in my own way: from the piano that often sits in my background until the moment comes when I feel ready to make music.

Introducing the performance, Swift says:

> I prepared [a mashup] for you that, if I was still on the Eras Tour, I would do this one. And it’s, um, songs that are connected to each other by the common theme of being about memory and reliving certain detailed memories of your life and the significance of those memories.

Music takes me through different moods and places, and the memories associated with each. The Grammy Museum performance takes me back to the Eras Tour, and all the hours spent by my piano learning different mashups, and back to when I played another Taylor Swift song with a friend on a public piano – a memory far away in space and time – and into the present, when I find the energy to sit by my piano, my laptop precariously perched on top, and, with a smile on my face, I begin to play the new combination of familiar songs in the Grammy Museum performance, translating what I hear, with my ear as my guide, into my own version of the mashup. Just as music brings back memories, so too does it create an opportunity to make new ones.

⁂ ⁂ ⁂

One day I would like to play guitar in public. I am nowhere near ready yet, but I can already imagine myself standing and playing, of bringing music to a quiet place, for a moment.

In addition to giving me an opportunity for me to sit by myself and spend time with music and songs I love, [music, when I perform in public places, is one of the ways I share my smile](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c39mlxl9d37o): a way for me to bring colour and sound and a little bit of happiness to a place, to a moment.

I hold onto the dream and the ambition, not of becoming a professional but becoming good enough to make someone else smile, or even sing along, just as I hope I can do when I play piano in public.
