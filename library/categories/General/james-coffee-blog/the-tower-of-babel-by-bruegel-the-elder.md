*Thomas and I are doing a collaborative writing challenge where we each choose a painting for the other to write about. Thomas chose The Tower of Babel by Pieter Bruegel the Elder for me to write about. You can read about the painting I chose for Thomas,* [*Meindert Hobbema’s Wooden Landscape*](https://www.vanderwal.net/random/entrysel.php?blog=2180)*, on his website.*

According to Wikipedia, Pieter Bruegel the Elder painted the Tower of Babel three times. Of the three paintings, two remain. For this challenge, I am writing about the painting held in the [Kunsthistorisches Museum in Vienna](https://www.khm.at/en/artworks/the-tower-of-babel-323). While doing research, I also learned that Pieter Bruegel the Elder’s son, Pieter Bruegel the Younger, also painted the Tower of Babel. (The Younger painted [copies of some of his father’s work](https://artuk.org/discover/artists/brueghel-the-younger-pieter-1564156516371638).) In doing research, I found a [record of sale of the Younger’s painting at Christies in 2022](https://www.christies.com/en/lot/lot-6377656).

Bruegel the Elder’s painting depicts The Tower of Babel being built, [a building in Genesis 11:1-9](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tower-of-Babel). In the story of the Tower, a group of people aim to build a large tower in Babylon. God sees what they are doing and then scatters the people and “[confuse[s] their language](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Tower-of-Babel)” so that they are unable to continue with the work. We see the incomplete tower in Brugel the Elder’s painting: the left side and foundation are well developed; much of the top of the tower, the largest point exceeding the height of a cloud, as well as the right side of the tower, are still under construction.

In the painting, the Tower of Babel dominates, taking up most of the centre of the piece. In the foreground, there are workers who are busy with stone, and several people bowing down to a man who is holding a gold staff. The workers to his left are toiling: using long poles (metal?) to help pull up a stone. To his right, in the background, there are people lying down on the grass, perhaps tired from all their work on the tower. (I don’t have all the context to properly analyse these details, so I’m sticking more with the visual analysis for this paragraph.)

I haven’t seen The Tower of Babel by Pieter Bruegel the Elder in person, and so for this blog post I am relying on images from the Kunsthistorisches Museum. My first impression of the painting was of the intense detail. Looking at the dimensions, the painting itself is 114.4cm x 155.5cm. It is huge! I can imagine this being the kind of painting where, in a museum, I would alternate between getting up close to appreciate details and then moving far away to appreciate the painting as a whole. Indeed, I have been doing this to some extent with the digital version, zooming in and out to appreciate small details and the larger work.

There are so many details to appreciate in this painting: the architecture of the tower itself; the individual workers who are discernible, such as one who is pointing upward near the centre of the painting and another nearby who is climbing a ladder; the individual buildings in the city in the background; the viaduct that surrounds the city; and more. Wherever I look, there is a new detail to appreciate: small houses and shelters around the rim of the third floor, the scaffolding that appears all across the tower, the contrast between the red stone in the centre and the white stone on the outside, how there is a castle to the right of the tower, at the bottom of the painting, that looks tiny in comparison to the tower. You can even see individual bricks in the top centre of the tower.

Looking closely at the city in the background of the right side of the painting, and comparing it to the left side, I noticed that the Tower casts a shadow on the left side of the city. This makes me think of how no matter how impressive the tower is, it has a significant impact on the city around it: parts of the city get darker; the city is in the background now; the tower takes up so much space.

One of the things that strikes me about this painting is the rocky slope in the foreground. Was the tower built next to a hill, and the rocky slope is left over from a natural landscape? Or was the slope constructed so as to allow for better access to the second floor of the tower and above? There is a similar slope in the top right of the painting, starting at the fourth floor, which is equally intriguing. It is an interesting feeling when you see something in a painting but don’t quite know how to decipher it.

Bruegel the Elder’s painting makes me think about how art can help us visualise stories. The painting of the Tower of Babel creates a vivid image of a building whose original story was recorded in text. It makes me think of all of the paintings that depict stories or figures from the [history of religion](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/54667?location[36063]=36063&periods[15]=15&periods[16]=16&periods[17]=17&page=0&search_set_offset=13) or [Antiquity](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/4760?location[36063]=36063&periods[15]=15&periods[16]=16&periods[17]=17&page=1&search_set_offset=99) or [allegories](https://www.nationalgalleries.org/art-and-artists/111059?location[36063]=36063&periods[15]=15&periods[16]=16&periods[17]=17&page=1&search_set_offset=79): every painting helps the viewer create an image in their mind of a scene, a place, a time.

In the case of Bruegel the Elder’s Tower of Babel, I am left with an impression of a huge, towering, work-in-progress structure that dominates the city in the background, and a reminder of the story that inspired this painting.
