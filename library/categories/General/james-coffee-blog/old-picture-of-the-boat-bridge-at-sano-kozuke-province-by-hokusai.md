[*Thamara*](https://thamara.co.uk/) *and I are doing an art exchange where we each sent the other person an art-related object to analyse. Thamara sent me a copy of “Beneath the Great Wave: Hokusai & Hiroshige”, a book from the art exhibition of the same name being held at the Whitworth in Manchester, from which I chose a single painting to write about. I sent Thamara my first greeting card in a set I recently purchased. The card has a painting on the front: Henri Edmond-Cross‘ The Pink Cloud.* [*You can read Thamara's post on his website*](https://thamara.co.uk/notebook/2026/08/the-pink-cloud)*.*

The [Old Picture of the Boat Bridge at Sano, Kozuke Province (1830)](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/53787) is a woodblock print by Katsushika Hokusai, who, most famously, painted *The Great Wave*, among other natural subjects. When I was looking through the book that Thamara sent, there were so many prints that appealed to me, but this one in particular stood out. I love winter scenes: of seeing how cold moments are depicted, and how colour is used to depict them.

![](https://editor.jamesg.blog/content/images/2026/08/DP141239.jpg)

*Image credit:* [*The Metropolitan Museum of Art*](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/53787)*.*

The Boat Bridge print features a bridge that spans across the left centre of the print. When I first looked at the print, I was focused on the surface of the bridge. But, then I wondered: how is this bridge, which looks relatively shallow, constructed? Looking closer, I realised the bridge was supported by boats floating in the water. Rope lines both sides of the bridge, a bridge strong enough to support both people and a horse who are traversing its path.

On the right of the print, a tree towers over the scene. From the perspective where the work was set, the tree is just as tall as the mountain in the background. The power of Nature was a theme in Hokusai’s work. *The Great Wave* showed a wave towering over both people and Mount Fuji. In comparison, the near-barren tree in this print is relatively peaceful. The mountain in the background, however, towers over the print. Of note, the tree is bending to the left, toward the bridge. Viewing this print, I do feel a bit of unease, from both imaging the way the bridge must sway when it is walked on, and the way the tree leans.

With that said, much of my attention is drawn to the colour of the print: white snow dominates the landscape, covering the mountains and hills in the background. Shades of blue, which are rendered using Prussian blue, a European pigment that was available in Japan from the 1820s onwards, appear throughout the middle of the print, the water darker at the bottom left of the print and lighter toward the top and on the right of the print. Studying the water, I see that the water around the bridge is turbulent, adding again to the sense of unease that this snowy scene depicts.

Indeed, it seems the more I think about this print, the more I see both movement and precariousness. I remember many snowy scenes evoking a peaceful mindset in me – the stillness of winter being what I remember. This print feels more alive: people are moving, the water is moving, the clouds are moving, the bridge is moving. It is even making me think back to Western snowy paintings I have seen and how there are often people moving too. It is amazing how one artwork can transform how I think of others.

Although there are only a few people in the print, I do feel like there is a sense of community: people are moving in the same direction, together, through the snowy landscape; dots of black and grey appear to denote footsteps where people have been walking. Each footstep is a sign of movement, too.

Despite how community comes through in this print (this print really does make me ebb back and forth, almost like the water itself is moving back and forth), I am curious about the two hats in the bottom foreground, which I assume represent people, distanced and obscured. We don’t see their faces. Now that I look more, I realise we don’t see anyone’s faces in the painting. I do wonder what the people in the bottom foreground are doing; they are more hidden than most.

There are two more parts of this print I want to point out. First, the clouds are different from any I have seen in Western art. The clouds look both heavy and light at once: heavy in that, on the left of the print, they are taking up a fair bit of space; light in that it looks like they are moving through the countryside. Second, the trees and shrubs are painted in blue. This makes the work feel somewhat surreal. Although, the blue does complement the white well, creating a beautiful effect.

I haven’t studied Eastern art to any great extent, beyond a short study of Van Gogh’s Bridge in the Rain (After Hiroshige), a painting based on Utagawa Hiroshige’s Sudden Shower over Shin-Ōhashi Bridge and Atake. Browsing through the book that Thamara sent me, I found so many paintings that make me want to learn much more about Japanese art: Hokusai’s Yōrō Waterfall in Mino Province, Hiroshige’s Night View of Matsuchiyama and the San’ya Canal, and more. I’m looking forward to spending more time with this book. Browsing through the book for an hour or so, I already ended up with four bookmarks.
