Every so often I hear or read about the significance of readers as part of the indie web. Indeed, the indie web is about much more than making personal websites: it is a space where there are many personal websites to explore. You don’t need a personal website to explore the indie web. And while having a personal website is a way to participate in the indie web, reading is participating, albeit, but in a different way. There are also middle points: for example, being a reader and starting a discussion (i.e. by emailing a site author, leaving a note in a guestbook.

I have spent time designing the experience for readers on this website, both in the visual sense as aforementioned but also in the collaborative sense. My site doesn’t have a comment form. Readers (that’s you!) are invited to email me. I link to my email address in my site navigation and often specifically invite readers to email me toward the end of a post. I write such invitations manually at the ends of posts so that they fit in with what my exact curiosity is in the moment.

I invite people to email me because I like to have longer-form discussions, and can do so without the discussion being public. There are limits, though: sometimes it takes me weeks to get back to an email from a reader because I am busy, and my inbox makes me feel a little bit anxious because I see the email to which I haven’t responded yet.

Of note, I now link to my /email page instead of including my email address inline in blog posts. This is so that if I decide to change my email address or communication preferences, I will not have to update all of the old email links.

I also have my guestbook, which visitors are invited to sign. The guestbook is more of a communal place to leave a message, and doesn’t create a direct communication: a signatory is not explicitly sending me an email to start a discussion, rather leaving a note for me and anyone else who comes to my site to see. This, too, is part of the reader experience for me.

A website may intentionally not allow comment in any way; for example, if the author just wants to share their thoughts without inviting a discussion. This, too, can be part of a designed experience for readers, one where the only interaction between the reader and the writer is what is published on the web.

While drafting this post, I used the term “reader experience” as my guiding light. This term encompasses every part of a site’s design: how people find blog posts and navigate between them, typography and colour choice, what is displayed on each web page and how it is arranged, and more. But the term “reader experience” also implies something related to how a reader can engage with a website, which helped open up the idea to, more generally, the author/reader interactions on the web.
