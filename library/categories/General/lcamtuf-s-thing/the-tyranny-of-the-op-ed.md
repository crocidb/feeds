I occasionally vent on social media about the comically lopsided incentives for authors who publish content on the internet. I try to keep these rants off the blog, apart from a [short piece](https://blog.coredump.cx/p/some-notes-on-influenceering) back in 2024 — but it’s been bugging me long enough that I should probably state my grievances more coherently.

It’s not too insightful to say that online discourse is strongly biased toward ephemeral, low-effort content. Complaints about this sound a lot like saltiness: it’s akin to getting upset that the rubes out there don’t appreciate the operatic voice of Angela Gheorghiu. Sucks, but the market has spoken; most people prefer to listen to Taylor Swift.

We could end it here, but my thesis is that the rebuttal is too reductionist: on the internet, Angela and Taylor are closer to Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde.

🙚 🙘

To illustrate, let me offer a personal anecdote: from 2010 to 2018, I ran a reasonably popular, infosec-centric blog. On that blog, I would often have opinions about current events. The opinions came to me effortlessly and resonated with the crowd: I could summon an army of tens of thousands of willing readers with a mere flick of my wrist.

I stopped writing in 2018 for work-related reasons. When I looked back at my grand opus in 2020, I realized that most of it was utterly worthless: the articles offered half-assed takes on long-forgotten vulnerabilities and incidents of the ancient past. I wasn’t changing minds or making friends. I was just padding other people’s social media feeds, the human equivalent of AI slop.

In a moment of anguish, I deleted most of it and decided to start anew. The articles I publish here take much longer to write and are meant to stand the test of time. But even on the new site, I can’t quite escape the specter of vacuous punditry. Some of my most top-ranking pieces on this site are spur-of-the-moment articles about a [real supply-chain hack in 2024](https://blog.coredump.cx/p/technologist-vs-spy-the-xz-backdoor) and another [imagined incident in 2025](https://blog.coredump.cx/p/investigating-an-evil-rj45-dongle). In retrospect, I shouldn’t have published them. The content stayed relevant for about five minutes; this week’s best-selling punditry product is the [Ledger supply chain attack](https://www.zetter-zeroday.com/who-is-planting-backdoors-in-ledger-crypto-wallets/). Still, the original view counts are there. They whisper in my ear, *“you know what, good things come to those who post hot takes about AI”*.

🙚 🙘

With this, I’m not trying to whine for attention: I don’t think I’m entitled to high view counts when I publish esoteric articles on philosophy or circuit dynamics. In fact, knowing that ten readers made it to the finish line of such a post feels more rewarding than having 10,000 people skim an essay about the Ledger incident, then promptly forget ever having been here.

What I worry about is the immediacy and the clarity of the feedback signal that writers get. It’s not a hypothetical: I *know* that I can easily get more views, more attention, more acclaim. I don’t need to break through, I don’t need to hone my style. Heck, I could probably ask an LLM to write the Ledger article — it’s not like Hacker News, LinkedIn, or Twitter will care. In other words: why be Dr. Jekyll when you can be Mr. Hyde?

I’ll grant that the emphasis on view counts is myopic, but writing is an inherently social endeavor; it exists in large part to pressure-test ideas and share them with the world. On the internet, engagement stats replace a more meaningful connection with an audience. Getting an email from a website visitor is a 1-in-a-100,000 happenstance. Whether authors admit it or not, *everyone peeks*.

🙚 🙘

So, what do we do about it? In theory, it’s easy to pinpoint the habit we ought to quit: doom scrolling fills up our time to the detriment of most other forms of human interaction, and the feed can only accommodate bite-sized thoughts. Even if you click to read the actual article — maybe one in a hundred? — you still only give it a minute or five before navigating away. The best the author can do is pander to your priors or get you angry about the misdeeds of the out-group.

I’m old-school, so I’m more used to treating the internet as a research resource. I come up with an idea I want to understand through-and-through, and then stay on its trail for days or even weeks; I move from one website to another, maybe even go on a walk to think it through. When you’re in that mode, distractions can wait: in a month, we’ll be still arguing about the same things (you guessed it: AI and politics).

But even among people with the same mindset, I think this usage pattern is on its way out: no matter how profound the inquiry, we increasingly lean toward ELI5 truths. We ask a chatbot, or we pull up a YouTube video that explains it with superficial analogies and pretty visuals.

Anyway — you kids need to get off my lawn, etcetera.
