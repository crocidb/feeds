Mike McIntire and Eli Murray, reporting for The New York Times (gift link)

>
>
> For nearly two decades, judges across the country have invoked this language to strike down gun-control laws. It is a pivotal legal test.
>
>
>
> It is also based on a misquote.
>
>
>
> Justice Antonin Scalia, [writing for the majority](https://www.nytimes.com/2008/06/27/washington/27scotus.html) in the 2008 case, District of Columbia v. Heller, attributed the “dangerous and unusual” language to Sir William Blackstone, an 18th-century English jurist whose writings became the basis for much of American law.
>
>
>
> But in his seminal 1769 treatise, “Commentaries on the Laws of England,” Blackstone referred to weapons that were “[dangerous *or* unusual](https://archive.org/details/bim_eighteenth-century_commentaries-on-the-laws_blackstone-william-sir_1775_4/page/148/mode/2up?q=unusual),” suggesting that a gun did not need to be both in order to be restricted.
>
>

[I’ve written before](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2018/02/16/nra-2nd-amendment) that *Heller* overturned two centuries of Supreme Court interpretation of the 2nd Amendment. The current interpretation — literally the NRA’s interpretation — was [characterized by former Chief Justice Warren Burger](https://daringfireball.net/linked/2018/03/27/stevens-2nd-amemdment) as “one of the greatest pieces of fraud, I repeat the word fraud, on the American public by special interest groups that I have ever seen in my lifetime.”

So it’s not like anything needs to rest on this and-vs.-or misquoting of something William Blackstone wrote before the United States was even founded. But, still, it’s interesting nonetheless how often the gun-fetish jurists like to cite the misquoting. The government should, and can, regulate dangerous weapons.
